## 1. A stronger reconstruction of de Souza's mechanism

**Explanandum.** Why can users operate every individual control correctly yet fail to understand the possibilities and strategy a designer has provided? De Souza illustrates that problem with a single-person Acrobat review workflow; she argues that usability checks focused on controls and operations can overlook the communication of the *value of a better task strategy* (**pp. 333–334**, especially the close of §2). This is an **illustrative author anecdote**, not a controlled experiment, independent audio sample or quantified treatment effect.

**Premise D1 — intellectual artefact, not neutral pipe.** The author treats software interfaces as linguistic encodings of designers' choices: users must interpret an introduced system of symbols, grammars and rules; computer artefacts differ from simple physical affordances (**pp. 318–321, §1.1**). The claim's broad scope is philosophical, not a measured universal result.

**Premise D2 — two linked acts, not merely output transfer.** In §3 (**p. 334**), de Souza distinguishes a designer's **one-shot** message about the interaction possibilities from the sequence of exchanges that can unfold at run time. “One-shot” refers to the *encoded, computer-processible design message*; in the same account it can be **progressively interpreted** by the user (**p. 335**). Do **not** infer that every generated alarm token is immutable, that all live states are predetermined, or that user comprehension occurs in one step.

**Premise D3 — real human meaning versus programmed interpretations.** On **pp. 337–338**, the article explicitly contrasts unpredictable, open-ended human semiosis with formal computational symbol processing. It argues that designers cannot completely precompute how a user will interpret a sign. This is an **ontological and epistemological commitment of de Souza herself**, rather than a neutral engineering theory that simply assumes human and machine meanings are interchangeable. The stronger comparison with Brier must acknowledge this shared concern while testing their different commitments.

**Premise D4 — social roles and revision are concrete inspection targets.** De Souza's analysis of Excel asks which **interlocutor** is being represented, who says “I” or “you”, how turn-taking is cued, how presuppositions/defaults are indicated, how explanations differ from actual negotiation, and how long a negotiated meaning remains valid or can be revised (**pp. 335–337, §3**). Her account is therefore *not merely* generic designer intent or an obsolete fixed dialog metaphor. It already poses **source attribution, turn order, default-assumption and temporal-scope questions** pertinent to the proposed MHAC foundation. The case is a **software-interface reading**, not a validated broadcast-alarm or natural-speech protocol; the figures were not visually inspected.

**Premise D5 — built-in epistemic limitations.** The article cautions that user purposes and interpreted meaning cannot be comprehensively captured at one time, and proposes methodological discipline to avoid both false certainty and total relativism (**p. 338, §3**). In §4 (**pp. 339–340**) the author further recognizes a challenge: users may not want to spend effort learning the designer's rationale; negotiation is attractive only when expected benefit justifies costs. This limits an unqualified proposal for more verbose machine explanation.

**Inferred mechanism (not the author's formal theorem).** Designed interaction paths embody assumptions; a user encountering a prompt must both interpret the current technical state and infer which communicative role and action affordance is intended. When those inferences diverge, delivering more technically accurate information may fail to repair the task misunderstanding. Because the proposed theory *already asks* about assumptions, authority-like interlocutor roles, revision and negotiation effort, an auditor must first test those capacities before crediting Brier with a unique applied contribution.

Brier's selected 2003 threshold discussion treats nonliving artefacts as quasi-/proto-semiotic with signification dependent on living interpreters (R007 memo, **pp. 81–83**). The AI-derived comparison cannot settle whether either proposed ontological demarcation holds for autonomous or learning machines; it is outside these selected texts.

### Counterfactual, with actual rejection condition

**Fictional analytical case only:** A machine reports “coolant hot; stop operation” to a shared workplace speaker. A contractor hears it, assumes they can reset a controller and the event changes during the attempted reset. There are *no participants, observations or collected measurements*.

1. **Strong no-Brier/no-de-Souza baseline:** ordinary state/provenance, addressee/authority, concurrent task, alarm legibility, versioned events, repair and affect-related questions. This is a *proposed checklist*, not a deployed policy.
2. **Add de Souza 2005:** scrutinize whether the interface represents the correct interlocutor, disclosed assumptions/defaults, intended action opportunities, revision of negotiated state and accessible explanation when misunderstandings matter (**§3, 335–337**). This may add *a different line of questioning* but must be tested for unique yield against the strong baseline.
3. **Add Brier:** separately require explanatory coverage of physical signal, lived experience, socio-organizational meaning and living interpretation. If these dimensions are already handled, **no Brier-specific design or predictive advantage has been demonstrated**.
4. **Pre-register a later controlled audit comparison (not performed):** same fictional scenarios, blind error rubric, conventional baseline versus baseline+de Souza versus baseline+de Souza+Brier. Count only independently adjudicated **new valid failure categories**, justified revised decisions and false-positive workload. A Brier-specific increment fails when its incremental findings are zero or semantic renamings of already detected errors. Even a successful paper audit would not establish auditory listener efficacy.

**Crucial negative result of the *argument* (not an empirical null result):** On the passages consulted, Brier is *not required* to justify source identity, turn-taking, negotiated-default auditing or meaning revision; de Souza already advances those questions. Conversely, de Souza does not settle embodied listener safety or supply a tested adaptive voice policy. A Brier-specific ontology might organize neglected disciplinary constraints, but whether that creates an empirical increment remains open.

## 4. Adverse cases, limitations and requested R006/R008 next source work

- **Designer versus operator vs sensor:** de Souza's first-person designer “I” does not make an alarm's immediate sensor or organizational authority a designer. Disclosure of *who asserts* a fact and *who can authorize* an action must not be collapsed into authorial intention. Brier's living-interpreter distinction likewise does not determine actual access control.
- **Unrequested negotiation can be harmful:** adding explanations/confirmation can increase delay, interruption, noise masking and alarm fatigue. De Souza §4 (**pp. 339–340**) acknowledges communicative effort trade-offs; the source supplies **no audio-specific quantitative threshold**.
- **Emotion-free wording does not guarantee neutral reception:** a monotone voice can be heard as cold or urgent; a prosodically expressive voice may improve intelligibility yet risk anthropomorphic attribution. These are *rival testable possibilities*, not measured results from either theory or Brier's endorsement.
