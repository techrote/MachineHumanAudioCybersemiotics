# R008 preparatory comparator — de Souza's designer–user metacommunication versus Brier's cybersemiotics

**Research question.** Does semiotic engineering already explain the designer's implied message about *how* a user can use a machine's message, such that Brier adds no distinct applied auditory-interface decision? Does semiotic engineering's own account overlook embodied, cultural or intersubjective phenomena that Brier can genuinely add, rather than merely rename?

## 2. Reconstruction of the *competing* explanatory account

**Explanandum — de Souza's account, not Brier's.** A user is not merely processing a computer's output; the interactive artefact also expresses what its designers believe users want, know, can do and ought to do. The 2005 peer-reviewed article's **publisher abstract** calls the interface a designer's deputy conveying a one-shot message about supported interaction. The 2010 **author text**, §1 printed **p. 2**, says the interface supports only interaction paths encoded at development time; the physical interface need not resemble a person for the design to communicate human choices. In §3 printed **p. 4**, the paper distinguishes interaction with a machine from *metacommunication*: the design communicates how communication with that system is to work. This can be analyzed as a human-to-human message *mediated* by an artefact, not evidence that the artefact itself has phenomenal understanding.

**Premise and mechanism, as reconstructed from the 2010 article (§§1, 3):**
1. Designers choose which tasks, users, interaction channels and permitted conversational paths the artefact supports (author claim, **p. 2**).
2. Those choices are expressed implicitly and explicitly in the interface, independent of designers being present during use (author claim, **pp. 2, 4**; 2005 article **abstract**).
3. A recipient must infer a model of available interactions from the representation, documentation and responses: successful decoding of a token or spoken sentence is not enough to communicate how the system should be used (our interpretation of **p. 4**, **not** a reported auditory result).
4. Therefore a communicability problem can arise when the user's inferred possibilities and the designed possibilities diverge (our inference). It can be scrutinised without committing to a metaphysical thesis about machine consciousness.

**Distinctive negative observation inside this primary source.** The 2010 paper's §4 (printed **pp. 5–7**) describes *the authors' own* small qualitative task: eight prospective volunteers designing Web Navigation Helper (WNH) dialogs to assist older users. The authors report that some volunteers inserted explanations lacking a basis in the website (example on printed **p. 7**). **This is an author-reported case, not an MHAC study, not a new independent sample, and not a numeric estimate of auditory performance.** It challenges the simplistic hypothesis that adding intermediary human-friendly explanations always improves fidelity: an intermediary can supply misinformation. The published **§5, pp. 7–8** also discusses the conflict between universal scalability and specific relationships of community responsibility. It is a *contextual source claim*, not a cross-cultural generalization supported by this bounded consultation.

**Strongest charitable version.** Semiotic engineering can ask both whether a notification communicates *state* and whether the interface communicates who its intended recipient is, what the recipient is enabled/authorized to do, and what kind of response is expected. This challenges a narrow input/output model before cybersemiotic terminology is added.

**Limits of that version.** De Souza's designer-deputy account is formulated for interactive artefacts and human intent; it does not establish that every autonomous machine state is a transparent expression of designer intent, that the designer can know each listener's situated context, that the user is a cooperative conversational partner, or that a spoken cue has a determinate emotional effect. The one-shot design message and a *live, changing* alarm are not the same event. Those are extensions to assess, not author-endorsed rules.

**Brier's separate explanatory target.** The successfully rendered Brier (2013) **p. 224 Figure 1** and **p. 225** locate meaning and knowledge in a plural framework encompassing physical, biological, first-person and intersubjective/social explanations; **p. 230** argues that no one disciplinary arm exhausts the others. The claim is at the level of an ontology/epistemology of cognition and communication. It is not the same as the inspectable engineering hypothesis that users understand the designer's intended interaction. Our concern is **not** whether two theories both employ Peirce but which *different explanatory work* is actually done.

**Category boundary:** de Souza's *designer-to-user metacommunication* must not be identified with Clark–Brennan's *mutual grounding criterion*, Harnad's *intrinsic symbol grounding*, Kolchinsky–Wolpert's *viability-relative semantic information*, a Shannon bit quantity or Brier's *semiotic praxis*. Similar uses of “communication” and “meaning” are not arguments for equivalence.

## 4. Remove-Brier and remove-de-Souza tests

**Fictional counterexample for analysis only, NOT a tested prototype or listener result:** A device truthfully detects excess heat, utters an urgent warning and reveals an action menu. The menu omits who may reset the controller and who must physically stop it. A competent operator hears the message but assumes the menu's reset command is authorized.

- **Conventional/no-Brier baseline.** Specify system-state provenance, addressee roles, action authority, available controls, false-assumption checks, acknowledgment/repair affordances, timing, and warnings about non-response. A communicability inspection can inspect whether the *designer's intended action model* is conveyed. Grounding and human-factors methods supply further rivals. This is a **proposed analysis checklist**, not a source-validated implementation prescription.
- **Add de Souza's lens.** Ask a distinct, inspectable question: *What account of the user, their tasks, permissible actions and meaning of interface elements is the designer already sending?* A control that silently invites unauthorized reset is itself a communicative failure even when its spoken status report is factually true. This is our extension from the 2010 **§3** metacommunication mechanism, not a conclusion from a voice experiment.
- **Add Brier's lens.** Ask whether the review has illegitimately ignored lived urgency, bodily response, organizational meaning or sociocultural conventions while optimizing telemetry alone. This is a **proposed four-perspective audit** inspired by Brier **pp. 224–225, 230**. The three inspected pages do not provide a novel audio policy or an additional prediction.
- **Remove Brier.** If a sufficiently mature de Souza + human-factors + affect/embodiment model still registers every distinct failure mode, repair decision and evaluable measure, the correct applied result is **“no unique demonstrated Brier increment.”** Richer ontological vocabulary alone is not a performance improvement.
- **Remove de Souza.** If an ordinary affordance/usability audit plus explicit designer-assumption checks fully captures the mistaken action authority, there is likewise **no independent de Souza increment**. Theory must earn a difference against a strong baseline, not a deliberately impoverished one.

**Potential unique benefit — bounded hypothesis only:** Brier's pluralism might prevent a *disciplinary omission* (e.g. a review process has formally excluded first-person fear or social authority as unscientific). But de Souza's 2010 cultural/accessible design discussion and ordinary HCI could also recover that dimension. Only a predeclared audit comparison with independent adjudication could test a genuinely unique additional *category of failure*; no such experiment or audit has been executed. A failure to identify any such category is an acceptable negative conclusion.

**Adverse cases to retain:**
- A “helpful” spoken explanation confidently invents an unsupported causal rationale. In the 2010 WNH source (**p. 7**), an analogous *written-dialog* phenomenon is **author-reported**, not an auditory result. Source-of-truth and evidence checking beat anthropomorphic warmth.
- A generic “neutral” machine voice is interpreted as indifferent in a hazardous context, while expressive prosody improves urgency recognition but also increases false attribution of machine emotion. Both possibilities are **unmeasured hypotheses**. The preference against performed emotion is *not* from Brier/de Souza, and affect attributed to the machine is distinct from a listener's own anxiety.
- A hearing-impaired or non-native listener does not understand a spoken alert even though the designed words and system-state proposition are correct. A purely designer-intent account is inadequate unless actual listener capabilities and acoustic/linguistic conditions are considered. Brier does not uniquely solve these.
- An alarm is broadcast to several operators who have conflicting duties. Neither the 2005 abstract's designer-deputy description nor selected Brier pages establish an actionable multi-actor authorization protocol.
