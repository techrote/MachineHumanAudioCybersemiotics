# The compass is a pronoun

*Original speculative essay for MHAC-X002. This is interpretation and fiction, not experimental, historical or anthropological evidence.*

Suppose an alien technical civilisation has made a very economical choice in language design. It owns one word for left. The word is short, unambiguous in its sound, reliable over radio and impossible to mistake for right. Every member of the civilisation can repeat it perfectly. No one has ever accused the language of lacking expressive precision.

Its engineers nevertheless keep filing incidents where correct instructions are executed incorrectly.

The difficulty is that the civilisation knows three centres. An operator has a left; a machine has a left; a plan engraved on a wall has a left. The language has a single acoustic symbol, so the listener hears *left* and silently provides the absent origin. The better the listener knows the word, the faster this supplementation becomes. A confident error is thus compatible with flawless hearing.

In a familiar room, the three lefts may point in the same direction. This coincidence reinforces a convention nobody thought to articulate. The grammar appears to work. Only when the machinery rotates does the hidden disagreement become visible. Nothing has changed in the sound. The room has changed the truth conditions of an action.

The important distinction is not between correct and incorrect pronunciations. It is between a word that can be *parsed* and a request that can be *executed*. The utterance has acquired a complete phonetic shape before it acquires an operational frame. A voice can be crystal-clear while its referent is not.

One faction of engineers wishes to fix the word by adding a new one: **panel-left**, **operator-left**, **machine-left**. Another argues that the whole civilisation should agree on a single default. A third says directions should never be relative at all: issue compass bearings and let every operator orient themselves. The factions disagree because each is solving a different problem: supplying metadata, establishing convention, or outsourcing the transform. None of the three solutions manufactures universal comprehension, physical orientation, sufficient time or permission to act.

An engineer trained on mathematical transformations notices an odd regularity. Rotate every actor and every apparatus by a quarter turn; every relative instruction's target rotates too. Its world action is not invariant, but the rule connecting action to its frame is. The engineer does not thereby discover a gauge theory of minds. It discovers the modest dignity of a typed relation: an instruction is not a bare direction, but a direction *in a frame*.

The civilisation eventually builds an instrument that speaks a tiny preface before a command. It can identify whether the command concerns a recipient, the equipment body or a wall display. The preface sounds nothing like a human voice. It might be a three-note motif, a brief spatialised click or an ordinary spoken phrase. The engineers refuse to bless the motifs on mathematical grounds. The choice between those sounds must survive real listening tests, with mistakes, hearing variation, masking and deadlines. A beautifully typed message is still useless if nobody hears its type.

At the same time a younger engineer discovers an uncomfortable exception. If the panel, speaker and recipient all face north, *left* needs no preface to determine the immediate action: all candidate directions are west. The costly ritual of always announcing the anchor would produce no extra action information in that scene. It may even delay a useful instruction. This is why the instrument must not confuse *a warning about possible ambiguity* with a moral requirement for more verbosity.

Then someone asks the question the mathematics did not answer: how does the machine know where the recipient is facing **now**? Its transform can be correct at emission and old at reception. The panel might have been moved. A display could have rotated. A human might be looking elsewhere. The frame label is true and the associated frame pose is false. The problem has merely migrated from missing reference to stale knowledge.

At the end of the thought experiment, a single word carries several quiet contracts: the frame is known, the transform is current, the intended actor is authorised, and the task can still be performed. No algebra licenses an agent to assume those contracts are satisfied because it can say the word confidently.

There is also a deliberate aesthetic opportunity. Imagine a synthesizer in which *notes* possess local coordinate systems. A motif means “rise” relative to a moving tonal centre, a second motif means “rise” relative to the stable register, and a third relative to another instrument's pitch. Turn the frame selector while keeping the motif unchanged. The harmony jumps without the gesture changing. One could make an instrument for hearing the difference between a constant instruction and a changing referent.

That is a compositional proposal, not a discovered universal sonic grammar. The experiment would begin in a synthesizer, not in an alarm system.

Perhaps the alien civilisation never had a word for error. Instead it had three: **the claim is contradicted**, **the reference is missing**, and **the response cannot yet be warranted**. Human machines could benefit from at least keeping these apart.

Our mathematical model only proves a small thing: if the same audible surface is compatible with several genuinely different required actions, no uniquely correct action can be computed from that surface and the supplied state alone. The philosophical reward is larger but less certain: sometimes the most honest response to an impeccably formed sentence is not disagreement and not obedience. It is a request for an origin.
