# The Drawing That Keeps Its Eraser
## An instrument made from the remains of its own subtraction

Refs #516 · 25 November 2026 · original exploratory humanities, mathematics and artistic research. Noncanonical, nonempirical, not an accepted interface requirement.

A musician deletes a note. Another note disappears in consequence. The final bank is empty. Is this the same empty bank that the musician opened this morning? As a playable inventory, perhaps. As a material object, as a record of labour, as an account of who affected whom, not necessarily. The proposition here is to make subtraction a compositional operation with its own material, without requiring a recording of what was removed.

## The frame is not a checksum

Robert Rauschenberg's Erased de Kooning Drawing (1953) invites a particularly instructive distinction between a blank sheet and an act of making something blank. Sarah Roberts's SFMOMA essay [S1] describes the role of the later frame and inscription, including Jasper Johns's contribution. The inscription makes a history available that cannot simply be read from the marks on the paper. The history is not equivalent to the visible residual material. Roberts also describes the embellishment of parts of the artist's repeated account; a framed account of an act is not immune to error.

The 1999 interview excerpt [S2] records Rauschenberg's retrospective explanation: erasing his own drawings had not achieved what he wanted, whereas he sought to begin with something whose artistic standing was already established. The collaborator's reported agreement was reluctant; this is Rauschenberg's recollection, not independently obtained permission evidence. The work cannot be reduced to a general permission to erase someone else's work. The museum's object entry [S3] describes digital enhancement of surviving marks in 2010 and cautions that de Kooning himself may have made some of the prior erasures. Even the residue is not a self-authenticating transcript.

Three different things thus coexist: a substrate altered by an act, a claim about the act and a social history in which the act has meaning. Our synthetic instrument can implement the first two; it cannot manufacture the third by assigning an ID.

William Kentridge's method supplies a different case. The Drawing Center's 1998 exhibition account [S4] explains how a charcoal drawing for a shot in Felix in Exile was modified through incremental addition and erasure. It situates his concerns with remembrance in South African history, and records his qualified account of the relation of History of the Main Complaint to the Truth and Reconciliation Commission. Kentridge's political and historical questions must not be abstracted into a decorative algorithm about memory. The narrow artistic borrowing is the possibility that a substrate and its successive revisions belong to the same ongoing work. The film's recorded frames and the surviving sheet are distinct historical objects.

Robert Morris provides an opposing preservation choice. Seattle Art Museum's entry for Box with the Sound of Its Own Making (1961) [S5] describes a wooden box containing a recording of its fabrication, including extended quiet periods. Here the process is represented by replay rather than inferred from the object's residual marks. A recording can carry more about the process than a deliberately designed scar, but its presence is also a temporal demand on the visitor. It may be the artwork's point to refuse the convenience of a summary.

These three constructions should remain distinct: the framed account of removal, an evolving substrate, and the replayed process. Neither a digital event log nor a residual statistic replaces all three. The new instrument is a fourth option: the author chooses which local consequences of an irreversible edit remain available to other synthesis operations.

## Exact toy: deletion leaves local relief

Take a fixed, known labelled simple graph G=(V,E). Initially all voices are present. Remove each vertex exactly once, in a strict total order. No additions, simultaneous edits, graph changes, missed events or uncertain counts are permitted. On removing v, set its scar s(v) to the number of its neighbours still present; the vertex's musical content is discarded. The scar is metadata at a known identity, not an attempt to recover content. Edges point from earlier to later removed vertices. Hence s is the labelled outdegree vector of an acyclic orientation, and sum(s)=|E|, independent of deletion order.

**Proposition.** Two removal histories leave equal labelled scars iff they agree on the relative order of the endpoints of every edge. Their entire total chronology may differ.

Proof: agreement on edges implies equality immediately. Conversely, a vertex with outdegree zero is a sink, so all incident edges point into it. Remove it from the graph, subtract one from each remaining neighbour's outdegree, then repeat. Every finite DAG has a sink. Induction forces the same edges in any two acyclic orientations with the given scar. A negative remaining count or lack of a sink means that the vector is not a valid complete deletion scar (assume integer counts, fixed graph and correct vector length). Conversely successful peeling constructs a DAG with the given counts. This is the reversed-arrow version of Corry and Perkinson's existing indegree uniqueness lemma [S6], not a new theorem.

Every acyclic orientation admits a topological ordering; any such ordering generates its scar. Thus the fibres of the scar map are precisely the topological orderings of the recovered orientation. Orientations are not necessarily causal: the graph was chosen by an artist, not learned from dependencies. A directed path entails relative order in this model, not physical influence or elapsed time.

For A—B—C, deleting A,C,B or C,A,B yields (1,0,1), with A→B and C→B. B was last; the relative order of A and C is unknowable from this scar. A,B,C yields (1,1,0) and C,B,A yields (0,1,1); both have the same sorted multiset (0,1,1). Dropping identities therefore destroys information even while preserving every individual count as an anonymous collection. Summing loses all chronology because every edge contributes once. On the four-cycle (1,1,1,1) passes the sum and degree bounds but is impossible for any deletion order: it lacks a sink. On a complete graph the scar values are n-1,...,0 in deletion order, revealing the entire order even though no event times are kept. This is a direct warning against calling a scar anonymous, private or forgotten simply because it is not a log.

For four voices every graph admits 24 total histories. The empty graph gives 1 distinct scar, a path gives 2^3=8 (all orientations of a tree are acyclic), a ring gives 2^4-2=14 (exclude the two directed cycles), and a complete graph gives 24. The composer can thus change the distinctions among histories without changing the final bank, the number of removals or the initial voice inventory. Edge count and chronology resolution are not identical in general. The result is about exact labelled vectors, not about whether an ear identifies them.

## The artistic fork: the eraser's register

The score (SCORE.md) uses a separate residue voice. Delete any active voice; in its labelled slot write a number of surviving neighbours. At the end the principal bank is empty. In canonical slot order, render each scar as a number of short impulses or as a new resonator parameter. The slot is necessary even for zero; an audible empty slot is not necessarily an inferable zero in an uncontrolled acoustic setting. For a strict synthetic render it is defined by a known score clock, not a new human-code claim.

A second mode can remove identities and play only the multiset. A third renders a single pulse per original edge, irrespective of the order. A fourth really discards the trace. These are distinct operations, not levels of artistic quality. The first is locally reversible with respect to edge order, not to deleted audio. The second and third can be deliberate forms of forgetting; neither makes a general guarantee of privacy.

An interesting compositional extension is to replace adjacency with spatial neighbourhoods of thousands of spectral grains. Erasing a grain may set a bounded new resonator amplitude from the number of still-active neighbours. The network remains fixed during a deletion pass but is freely redesigned between passes. For efficient large grids, update live degrees of neighbours when a node is removed; no full event log is required by the toy. Preserve a separate audio bypass and a true no-residue option. A second instrument could use the residual orientation as a feed-forward modulation topology after the original sounds are gone. Reinsert only explicitly new voices; do not mislabel this process undo.

This is a proposed design, not a demonstrated audio engine. Conventional graph scheduling, edit metadata and sample routing suffice. If existing editors make the effect equally easy to author, the contribution is a score and a way of thinking, not a new software requirement.

## Counterargument: the blank sheet should sometimes stay blank

A permanent trace is not a universal virtue. The frame in Rauschenberg's work is part of a particular artistic decision. It does not authorize the automatic preservation of everybody's removed material. The materiality of Kentridge's revisions is also not a general claim that all records should survive a deletion. A scar may be unwanted surveillance, even where no source content survives. In this model a complete graph discloses the whole sequence of removal identities. A graph with one edge discloses only one comparison. The meaning of forgetting depends on which question can still be answered.

A design for MHAC might distinguish explicit deletion, no prior content, and inaccessible history only when a trustworthy source supplies that distinction. A valid scar can support a narrow comparison, not an identity, motive, historical timestamp or actual deletion of external data. Any claim of retention or deletion must match the implementation; do not imply deletion of backups, inaccessible data or other people's memories. For ordinary communication, a simple log or clear status may outperform a scar. An eventual consented synthetic study could compare log, labelled scar, anonymous scar and no-trace views on edge-order inference, nonedge-order overclaim, and inspection cost. The acoustic representation would require separate testing; no human efficacy is inferred from the graph theorem.
