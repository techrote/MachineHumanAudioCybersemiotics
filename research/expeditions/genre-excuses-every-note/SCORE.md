# Original performance score: The Genre That Excuses Every Note

For two pitches, two interpreters, and one scorekeeper. No human research data collection; this is an artistic instruction, not an experiment protocol.

**I. Two schools.** Set A=220 Hz, B=330 Hz, 250 ms slots. Start on A; play transitions 00001111 (repeat four times, then change four times). All notes have identical duration and envelope. Interpreter R expects repetition; interpreter C expects change. Both may tap a separate unpitched surface on events their respective rule disfavors. Neither may stop the music.

**II. Equal inventory.** Play transitions 01010101, with the same initial A. The critics will have the same number of disfavored events as before if they retain their original grammar. The order of the taps changes. With one authorized change of school, the first phrase can be rendered entirely unsurprising to its declared grammar, whereas the second cannot. In this movement choose the time of the switch before performance, then repeat with hindsight. Discuss what changed: the sound, the expectation, or the description?

**III. The accommodating critic.** Permit a third interpreter to choose R or C after every event. They must always choose the grammar favoring the event. They are forbidden to tap. Repeat both scores. The silence of this critic now distinguishes neither score from any other eight-transition word. Give the silent critic a new instrument: instead of sounding mismatches, strike a bell whenever they change school. The initially silent interpretation acquires a rhythm.

**IV. Refusal of the category.** Let the two performers improvise with duration, spectral texture and silence. The interpreters must now decide whether the binary score can represent what they hear at all. An interpreter may abstain rather than assign a grammar. The score ends when the first interpreter admits that an event is interesting for a reason the system cannot encode.

## Fiction: Museum of Well-Behaved Music

The Museum of Well-Behaved Music acquired an instrument whose notes were always right. Its predecessors had been much more troublesome: occasionally a note would violate the rules of its genre, and the curators would spend whole mornings debating whether to mend the instrument or retain the deviation.

The new instrument was different. Its curator could change the genre between notes. Every sound arrived equipped with a small certificate of belonging, dated a fraction of a second after the sound itself.

The visitors initially admired the certificates. A difficult passage could finally be explained. After a few years the museum discovered that it could certify any recording whatsoever. Its archive of masterpieces was indistinguishable, by the museum's own criterion, from its archive of faulty doorbells.

An apprentice proposed a second collection. It would preserve not only the recordings and certificates but the moments at which the curator had changed their mind. The new exhibition attracted composers who had never been interested in the original music. They came to hear the curator.

The museum installed a bell for every change of school. The curator began to resent the bell. It made it difficult to tell whether he was interpreting the instrument or playing it.

In the last room there was a recording with no certificate. A sign explained that the museum had not yet found the right category. It did not say that the recording had failed.
