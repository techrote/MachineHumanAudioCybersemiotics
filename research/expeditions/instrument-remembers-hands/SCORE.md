# Four keys, two biographies
Original text score, speculative artistic research. No performance or human study conducted.

Use four distinguishable pitches, e.g. C4, E4, G4, B4, and four accessible actions selected by the performer. A partner or software maps actions to pitches. This is a concert exercise, not a test of bodily ability. Actions may be keyboard keys, switches, eye gestures, voice commands, or an agreed alternative. Use any comfortable volume; no time or strength requirement. Opt out of concealed changes whenever desired.

**Movement I — Dwelling.** Randomly assign a bijection and leave it unchanged. Begin without a score. The performer may explore until they choose to stop. Compose a four-event motif, then a variation. Retain both gestures and pitches. The listener may record the pitches, not the gestures unless the performer consents.

**Movement II — The substitute.** Disclose that the controller's mapping will change, without announcing which two associations will be swapped. The performer may first replay the familiar motif without attempting correction, or skip directly to exploration. Let the difference between intended and produced sound enter the composition. Do not call the resulting sound a mistake unless the performer does.

**Movement III — The translator.** Supply an inverse mapping adapter or negotiate a new controller that the performer finds usable. Repeat the familiar motif and then improvise. The performer may keep, alter or reject the adapter. Listen for a new relationship rather than trying to prove that the previous relationship is restored.

**Movement IV — Two histories.** One partner changes mappings only between sections; another changes them unpredictably between whole phrases. The performer can request a fixed mode, a visible mapping, or a reset at any point. These are part of the score, not cheating. End with the same four pitches in the same order as the opening composed motif if desired. If the motif did not use every pitch, repeat its actual pitch sequence instead.

The audience hears the notes, but only the player can describe the role the apparatus played in acquiring them. The notes do not authenticate that description. The score is meant to invite reflection, not to extract or generalize data.

## Speculative miniature: The Piano of the New Republic

All instruments in the public conservatory were replaced every December. The Department of Equivalence maintained that this was not a replacement at all: each new machine possessed the same inventory of sounds as its predecessor.

In the first year the students learned to count their fingers. In the second year they learned to count the days until December. By the third year the annual concert consisted almost entirely of pieces written for one note.

A repairer, asked to remedy the shrinking repertoire, offered not a better oscillator but a little card on which each musician could keep a mapping. It was not a compulsory card. Some chose to spend December learning again; others selected different gestures entirely. The oldest player took a card home and returned it blank. Her instrument would change, she said, when she chose to change it.

The Department published a list of the unchanged pitches. The conservatory published a different list: the things its musicians no longer needed to learn twice.
