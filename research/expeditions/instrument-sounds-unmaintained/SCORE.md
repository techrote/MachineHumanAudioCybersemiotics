# Service Counterpoint — a score for three musicians and a witness

Original artistic research, not instructions for operating real machinery. An entirely simulated or ordinary musical instrument suffices. No real fault, unsafe operation, covert recording or unwilling labour is required.

## I. Two identical concerts

The first musician selects eight notes and plays them without variation. A second musician is given the disturbance sequence 1,1,1,0,0,0,-1,1. Before each note the third musician may negate the disturbance's accumulated sum, provided the magnitude of their correction does not exceed four.

Repeat the concert with no disturbance. The audience should not be told which version occurred until both have ended. If the model is used, both versions are literally identical at the output. If humans perform the score, equality is a musical aspiration, not a measured fact.

## II. The service part

Repeat the disturbed concert. The maintainer may mark a correction movement by a short high tone. Alternatively, the maintainer may reset the simulated disturbance before the note, marking the reset with a short low tone. The public melody remains unchanged. The witness records both versions but is prohibited from calling the number of tones the amount of labour.

Give the maintainer the right to omit the optional artistic accompaniment. The audience is not owed a spectacle of care.

## III. The reserve

Stop at the end of the third note. The witness displays the remaining correction range, but not the identity of the maintainer. Offer two further positive disturbance ticks to the compensated and reset versions, with no further reset permitted. The first reaches a note it can no longer restore; the second does not. The performer may let the detuning become music, or stop. The witness must distinguish an unavailable repair from a refusal to perform.

## IV. The rehearsal without a concert

The principal musician leaves the room. The other two rehearse maintenance actions on the imaginary instrument without emitting the principal melody. If they choose, the service lane may sound on its own. The audience can leave and no one must remain to certify the rehearsal's artistic worth.

## Afterword: The Department of Unchanged Things

The city owned two instruments that had played the same note since the railway opened. The annual report described the first as perfect and the second as defective because it employed a tuner.

When the tuner retired, the report was revised to describe both instruments as equally economical. For several days the notes agreed.

A clerk discovered a small number in the second instrument, decreasing every morning. It was not the pitch. She rang the department.

The department asked why she had called when nothing had changed.

She answered that the number of ways in which nothing could change had changed.

The city commissioned a bell that rang every time the tuner worked. The tuner objected: she had agreed to maintain an instrument, not to provide a permanent performance of her employment.

The bell was installed in a drawer. The instrument could continue to sound unchanged. The drawer could be opened by agreement.

At the next concert, the programme had room for the name of the tuner without requiring the bell to ring.
