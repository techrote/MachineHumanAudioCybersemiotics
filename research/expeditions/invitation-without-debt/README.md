# The Invitation That Does Not Become a Debt

**Expedition #390 · 2026-11-24 · exploratory humanities, formal construction and artistic research.** Not a canonical evidence record, protocol amendment, claim of novelty, or validated interaction recommendation.

## 1. A note that someone was entitled not to play

A conductor reserves four beats for a clarinettist. The player lets the interval pass. A different conductor reserves no beats for that player. In both recordings the clarinet is absent. The absence in the first may belong to the performer; in the second it belongs to the arrangement. A recording cannot in general adjudicate the difference.

This is not an argument for more silence. Silence can be coerced, inadvertent, attentive, inaccessible, or an expressive refusal. It is an argument for composing the *availability* of an action separately from the action itself. An invitation that insists on audible proof of gratitude may be structurally closer to a demand. A musician who never sounds could still have exercised a privilege throughout a performance.

Call the proposed practice **permission counterpoint**. One line of the score states who may sound; a second states who actually sounds. Neither is reducible to the other. A third line, if wanted, describes requests or obligations. The term is a local artistic construction, not a claim to have discovered a new ethical category.

## 2. Intellectual encounters and disagreements

**Sacks, Schegloff and Jefferson (1974).** “A simplest systematics for the organization of turn-taking for conversation,” *Language* 50(4), 696–735, DOI [10.2307/412243](https://doi.org/10.2307/412243). [Accessible primary PDF](https://www.conversationanalysis.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/06/04_Sacks_Schegloff_Jefferson_A_simplest_systematics_fir_the_organization_of_turn-taking_for_conversation_Language_.pdf). Read introduction p. 696 and §3.3–3.4 pp. 704–705; inspected image of printed p. 704 (PDF index 8). Their ordered rules distinguish selection by the incumbent, self-selection and continuation by the incumbent. A selected conversational participant has both an entitlement and an obligation to take the next turn under rule 1a. In contrast, self-selection under 1b is optional. The rules are a proposed description of conversational organization, not a moral prescription for all human communication. Their opening treatment of turn allocation as an economy is especially germane. **Interpretation:** the equivalent of a note in a conversational score could be a change in the distribution of next-turn rights, not a sound. **Departure:** this expedition explicitly splits invitation from obligation; it does not rename rule 1a an optional invitation. We do not infer a universal conversational grammar.

**Christian Wolff interviewed by James Saunders (email 2004–05, published 2009).** [Interview](https://www.james-saunders.com/interview-with-christian-wolff/), excerpt credited to *The Ashgate Research Companion to Experimental Music*, pp. 359–368. Read the opening exchange on contingent processes, particularly the paragraphs beginning with his practical motivation for a 1957 two-piano piece and the example of waiting for a colleague's sound to stop. Wolff describes indeterminacy as arising in part from practical limitations and reciprocal effects between players, and the possibility of political exemplarity without subordinating music to a political message. His stop-triggered cue is a constraint: one player's event determines the other's timing. **Interpretation:** a sound can address another performer rather than merely reach an audience. **Counterpoint:** tightly enforced responsiveness can be an engrossing musical constraint, and there is no reason that every score ought to maximize opt-out freedom. A voluntary ensemble may adopt binding musical rules. Formal equality of parts does not demonstrate social equality. The artistic object here is the difference between a cue that triggers and a cue that permits.

**Pauline Oliveros, The Tuning Meditation.** [Rubin Museum reproduction](https://rubinmuseum.org/a-legacy-of-sound-sonic-meditations-by-pauline-oliveros/), section “The Tuning Meditation” including its commentary; read the complete reproduced instructions. Its alternation between producing an independently imagined tone and matching a distant participant situates action in listening. Its ensemble is not modelled as a monophonic turn scheduler. **Interpretation:** a relationship to another voice can be the content of a compositional gesture. **Counterexample:** exclusivity is not intrinsically virtuous; a mutually responsive texture may require overlapping sound. This expedition's slots are a deliberately reduced laboratory, not a replacement for the overlapping auditory field of Oliveros's practice. We neither endorse therapeutic claims nor reproduce her score.

**Mees van Hulzen (2021), “Gratitude and that which we cannot return: Critical reflections on gratitude,” *Zeitschrift für Ethik und Moralphilosophie* 4, 109–119, [DOI 10.1007/s42048-021-00091-x](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s42048-021-00091-x).** Read abstract, §2.2 “Ceremonial Gift-Giving,” §2.3 “Charity,” and conclusion excerpts. His account of Mauss identifies obligations surrounding giving, receiving and returning, and he contrasts gratitude as repayment with gratitude as recognition and responsibility. He also considers how gratitude can serve submission when a recipient cannot afford to refuse a benefactor. This is philosophical argument, not an experiment measuring indebtedness. Mauss and Hénaff are encountered through van Hulzen's discussion, not claimed as independently read primary texts. The article itself criticizes elements of Mauss's treatment of Māori beliefs; no ethnographic inference is drawn here. **Interpretation:** a supposedly generous interface can use its gifts to impose a recurrent obligation to respond. **Objection:** a relationship without any responsibility to others is not necessarily a desirable relation. The local aim is not freedom from every obligation; it is to distinguish obligations from optional opportunities rather than smuggle one into the other.

These four sources do not converge on a common theory. Conversation analysis describes organization; Wolff and Oliveros construct different musical practices; van Hulzen argues about the ethics of recognition. Their productive disagreement concerns whether a response is structurally expected, artistically constitutive, freely offered, or socially indebted.

## 3. A finite counterexample: balanced concerts, unbalanced opportunities

**Formal construction.** Let there be m named performers and n known slots. Exactly one performer is offered exclusive access in each slot; they may sound or remain silent. The observer sees identity when a sound occurs and a silence otherwise. No other state or cue is observed. Perfect symbolic detection, no unauthorized sounds, no overlap, no scheduler constraints, and no probabilistic assumptions. Silence does not identify whether a cue was received or consciously declined; the model only tracks offer and uptake.

Let r_i be observed sounds by performer i, and s the number of silent slots. An offer count vector o is compatible exactly when o_i >= r_i and sum(o_i)=n. Set d_i=o_i-r_i; the d_i are nonnegative and sum to s.

**Proof.** Sounding slots fix their offered identity. Every silent slot can independently be assigned any of m identities. For a specified d, select which silent slots go to each performer; every such assignment is compatible. Thus there are m^s distinct offer histories for a fixed audible trace and s!/(d_1! ... d_m!) histories with a specified count vector. There are binomial(s+m-1,m-1) possible count vectors (stars and bars). This is elementary combinatorics; no mathematical priority or psychological model is claimed. Counts do not define a probability distribution unless an additional prior is adopted.

For two performers A and B, the signed difference in offered turns lies in the exact arithmetic progression from r_A-r_B-s to r_A-r_B+s in steps of two. Both endpoints are attained by assigning all silence to one performer. If n is divisible by m, equal offered counts are possible precisely when all r_i <= n/m: assign each performer their remaining quota from the silent slots. Note that this is feasibility, not evidence that a balanced schedule occurred.

Example with n=8: audible A, B, then six rests. There are 64 possible offered schedules. Exactly 20 have four offers to each performer. A 7:1 allocation in either direction is also compatible. Both heard performers contributed the same number of notes. This is an exact observational ambiguity, not a fairness metric.

Conversely, an equal offered schedule does not guarantee an equal sounded schedule; one participant may take every opportunity and another none. A scheduler cannot both require equal positive output against all possible refusal patterns and leave uptake entirely voluntary. The all-refuse case is sufficient to disprove the guarantee; more interestingly, one willing and one refusing player defeats any strictly positive equal quota without suppressing the willing player's contribution or manufacturing participation for the other. Equal output can sometimes be achieved by muting willing performers, but that is not equal positive participation.

The construction does not establish that actual offers are equally accessible, equally valuable, or equally freely refused. A performer given a silent invitation they cannot perceive has not necessarily been given a usable opportunity. Counting offers is not counting agency. A policy with auditory-only cues may have a very different effect from one with visual or tactile cues. The laboratory's assumptions intentionally exclude these issues so the nonidentifiability is inspectable.

**Executed verification:** the accompanying oracle.cjs enumerated every schedule/uptake history for m=2, n=0..8 and m=3, n=0..6. It grouped histories by audible trace, independently enumerated weak compositions of silent slots, compared all multinomial counts and tested balanced feasibility plus the sharp two-player disparity. 143,368 histories; 15,302 trace groups (across configurations); 111,956 assertions passed. The exact source function was executed in a JavaScript tool environment, serialized to the committed file, and fetched content compared equal. Run with node oracle.cjs. For the example the multiplicities for A offers 1 through 7 are 1,6,15,20,15,6,1. No statistical or participant data.

## 4. Design hypothesis: the permission bus

A conventional sequencer represents a rest as a non-event. A permission sequencer could represent the rest as an event at the access layer even when no synthesis is requested.

Proposed data dimensions: (1) offer identity and issuing authority, (2) recipient, (3) validity window, (4) exclusive/shared eligibility, (5) optional/required status, (6) observed uptake, (7) independently confirmed decline if explicitly supplied, (8) expiration, (9) possible transfer. Do not infer consent or refusal from silence. For ordinary artistic use, an expired opportunity need not be carried forward as a debt. Repeated re-offering can itself be a coercive design choice; rate limits and a genuine no-further-offers control should be considered. An explicit decline must not secretly trigger an equivalent request under another label.

An instrument could separate the oscillator bank from the gate-permission bank. A performer can modulate permissions without triggering an oscillator. A partner can audition permissions as a separate sound layer, or keep them in a private non-auditory control plane. A score can redistribute opportunities through the piece while retaining an invariant sounded motif. Private declines need not be exposed to the public. Group-facing logs can disclose aggregate allocation only with appropriate agreement; aggregation may itself reveal a participant in small groups.

Consider three policies: fixed cyclic invitation; invitation to the least recently *offered* participant; invitation to the least recently *heard* participant. The third may continually re-address a performer who chooses not to play; the first two can move on without treating silence as debt. This is a mechanism-level prediction conditional on those rules, not a claim that participants would prefer any policy. On the other hand, a performer whose quietness arises from exclusion or inaccessible cues might benefit from an alternative offer. Distinguish chosen abstention from unverified delivery; do not let the scheduler infer preference solely from lack of sound.

For MHAC, distinguish optional prompts, mandatory action requests, acknowledgement, actual action and resolution. Optional prompts are a possible place to test non-punitive expiry; operational obligations must remain visible in appropriate channels and escalation must not be disabled by this artistic analogy. A proposed synthetic study can compare offer-expiry with repeated re-prompting and explicitly separate cue perception, intention, authority and uptake. Later, properly authorized human studies could measure comprehension of optionality, unintended compliance, missed genuine obligations, perceived pressure, accessibility, and notification burden. Reject the design if it increases missed required actions or systematically miscommunicates the status of an offer. No real-world tests or recruitment performed.
