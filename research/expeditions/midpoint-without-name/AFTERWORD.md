# The School of Equal Distances

*Original speculative fiction; not a historical or empirical claim.*

The city had two bells. The first rang at the beginning of a journey and the second at its end. Between them lay the School of Equal Distances, whose purpose was to train people to recognize halfway.

On Monday the students were given a wooden lever. On Tuesday it was replaced by a glass lever of exactly the same length. No one could tell why Tuesday's halfway note was higher.

The school possessed certificates for both instruments. The certificates described the first bell, the last bell, the direction of travel, and the uninterrupted continuity of the mechanism. Both were correct.

An apprentice took the glass lever apart. Inside was a curved track. She put the track beside Monday's straight one. She was reprimanded for displaying the machinery during a music lesson.

The following winter a composer arrived. She asked whether the city had any unemployed curves. The instrument makers offered her a box full of rejected calibration profiles. She installed the box beneath a keyboard and gave its controls to a second performer. For the first time two people could play the same journey: one decided where it began and ended, the other decided where its middle would be.

The school certified the performance at both ends. Its inspectors had no box in which to record the rest.

Years later the composer was asked to repair the curriculum. She did not remove arithmetic. She added two questions to the examination: halfway in what, and for whom?

The wooden and glass levers were displayed in adjoining cases. The labels contained the same measurements, but the cases were connected to different loudspeakers. A visitor could move both handles with one hand and listen to the divergence.

In the city archive the certificate of the first bell was still perfectly legible. So was the certificate of the last bell. Between them the registrar left an empty page, not because nothing had happened there, but because she had finally learned not to certify a distance she had never measured.
