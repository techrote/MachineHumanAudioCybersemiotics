# The auditorium with two doors

*Original speculative fiction. Not a historical report or empirical result.*

The hall had been built for a single listener. There were two doors behind the stage, a screen between the stage and the chair, and eight bronze switches on the armrest.

The archivist explained that the eight sounds were always the same. She could offer a guarantee stronger than resemblance: the electric impulses delivered to the loudspeaker would be identical, no matter which orchestra she invited to stand behind the screen.

Through one door came four players who performed the first half of the work; through the other came four who performed the second. On a later evening they alternated. Their instruments were identical machines, although the people were not. The composition did not contain their names.

At first the listener found the arrangement disappointing. Nothing about the room was changed by the people who entered it. The sound possessed an almost bureaucratic indifference to its causes.

Then the archivist opened a second panel. It was labelled "Silence the first door." He pressed it. The first four sounds disappeared.

The following evening he pressed the same switch, at the same time, after listening to the same music. This time every other sound disappeared.

He began to suspect that the two orchestras had not performed the same composition. The archivist refused to adjudicate the matter. She offered two catalogues: one indexed the sound delivered to the hall, and the other indexed the possible consequences of its controls. The two catalogues used different numbering systems.

Years later the old hall became a museum. The stage machinery had been lost; the recording of the piece was perfect. Its donors paid for a reconstruction, and the conservators built a machine that could play all thirty-five balanced orchestrations. They could change one into another without disturbing a single sample in the master output. No one knew which one had actually been played at the premiere.

They installed the machine where the original switch had been. Its label did not say "original orchestra." It said "thirty-five ways to be silent."

A visitor asked whether this meant the music was incomplete.

The archivist answered that the recording was complete. The question was not.
