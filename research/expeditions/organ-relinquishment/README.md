# The Organ That Gives Away Its Keys

**Expedition #616 · 25 November 2026 · speculative art / source-located interpretation / synthetic apparatus · Draft/HOLD.**

## 1. The commission

Imagine a concert organ that begins with every pipe under the authority of a machine. Its first composition is excellent, but its second composition is the gradual removal of its own privilege to compose the first. A visitor may take responsibility for a pipe, decline the invitation, or leave it quiet. The organ may also withdraw without finding a replacement. Its eventual silence is not necessarily an error, nor is it evidence that the visitors agreed.

This is a proposed artwork about relinquishment, not a claim about machine consciousness. A machine cannot give moral ownership merely by setting a flag; the word "guest" denotes an explicitly stipulated musical role.

Ordinary collaborative instruments often add another voice to a mix. The interesting alternative is to compose the decreasing reach of the original voice. A finite resource (keys, resonators, grains, spectral territory, or performance time) can become available without requiring an audience member to become a performer. This makes vacancy a first-class compositional object, not an embarrassing hole to be filled by automatic accompaniment.

## 3. Interpretation: the artist who still owns the invitation

The Harburg column's withdrawal is materially visible over a period of years. Yet the artists still determine the architecture of participation: the means of inscription, the surface available and the mechanism of descent. The work questions monumental authority without magically abolishing authorship. The historical and political specificity of the work must remain intact.

Hoheisel's negative form offers a different lesson. The visible solid object is absent, but water renders a hidden space audible. Negative form is not acoustic silence; the work can acquire a new presence precisely through the design of its absence. For a musical instrument, this suggests a distinction between subtracting an oscillator and providing a resonant space made available by its withdrawal. A resonator could be excited by a visitor, or remain unexcited. It should not produce a counterfeit visitor when nobody elects to use it.

Lozano-Hemmer's work supplies the decisive counterexample to a sentimental account of participation. An incoming contribution can displace an earlier contribution. A system may become more participatory by one definition while reducing the duration of an individual's presence. The artist makes that tension material; it is not necessarily a defect. An organ might offer two modes: a finite circulating gallery that rotates contributions, and a retreating instrument that gives away its own remaining positions but never evicts a guest. They are different compositions of finite capacity.

Three things must not be confused: the disappearance of the object, the accessibility of the newly opened space, and the distribution of power over what happens next. An empty stage may be inaccessible. An open stage may be dominated by one performer. A quiet machine may still retain all decisions. Relinquishment must be inspectable at the level of actual routing and control, not inferred from a moving fade slider.

There is an artistic objection to making these things inspectable. An installation may lose its mystery if every silence has a label. The proposed resolution is not to impose a permanent diagnostic display on artistic experience, but to permit independent inspection of rules and roles. A work can contain unlabelled ambiguity while its accompanying apparatus truthfully describes how it was produced.

## 4. Finite construction and limits

Three labelled seats begin in M (machine occupied). O is offered, with machine sound retained; G is accepted guest occupation; V is intentional vacancy. Legal transitions:

| Action | From | To |
|---|---|---|
| Offer | M | O |
| Decline | O | M |
| Accept | O | G |
| Withdraw | M or O | V |
| Release | G | V |

The function K = number of M or O seats is nonincreasing. Offer and decline leave K unchanged; acceptance and withdrawal reduce it by one; guest release does not change it. Every seat changes independently, and V is terminal in this particular performance. G cannot be removed by a machine offer; release is a distinct guest action in the fiction. Nothing forces a guest to emit sound. In the simple renderer G is scripted with a different tone. There is no identity authentication or access-control security in this model.

A consequence: for n seats initially in M, at most n acceptances can occur before reset; no algorithm can promise unbounded admission with finite seats, permanent retirement and no eviction. This is a property of the stipulated transition graph, not a theorem about participation. A recurrent variant may reopen seats, employ time-sharing, or rotate guests. These are new policies with different consequences, not optimizations that silently preserve the original contract.

A distinct occupancy vector and its acoustic output need not be in one-to-one correspondence. M and O are rendered identically. V is quiet, but a muted guest could also be quiet in an expanded model. The presence of sound or the lack of it cannot by itself establish an invitation, a transfer, or agreement. The supplied laboratory checks reachable states and rejected transitions in a finite three-seat model; it renders four small synthetic compositions. It does not test real humans or real-time software.

## 5. Exaptation and dissent

The Relinquishment Organ VSTi could begin with a persistent orchestra of autonomous oscillators. MIDI input requests musical territory; a separate explicit gesture accepts a proposed handoff. A transfer operates on a bounded voice allocation, not on audio amplitude alone. A guest may release or mute a region without being silently replaced. A separate mode may permit rotation, provided that the score makes its eviction policy clear. For DAW use, no simulated authority should control real permissions; the whole system is a musical sandbox.

A companion Receding Accompanist VSTfx could gradually reduce generated accompaniment in explicitly selected time-frequency regions while leaving the dry input untouched. Do not infer that a spectral gap is consent or absence of a human. Optional visual inspection of offered / occupied / vacant state, a fixed dry bypass, click-free fades, bounded voice counts and an external stop are appropriate implementation constraints. Audio threads should not allocate or perform file I/O. These are implementation proposals, not qualified VST binaries.

An alternative installation uses an invisible resonator under each retired slot. Sound enters only through an intentional performer gesture. Its architecture owes a metaphor to Hoheisel; it is not a memorial to the history his work addresses. A composer may prefer the fully automatic organ, the continuously overwritten pulse gallery, or the unreplenished silent space. No universal ethical ranking follows from this model.

For MHAC, a narrow design hypothesis is that distinguishing OFFERED, ACCEPTED, RELEASED and VACANT in a simulated interface may reduce erroneous inference of handoff relative to sound-only withdrawal. An experiment should compare explicit text status, audio-only, and combined presentation using synthetic musical tasks, record false transfer and refusal attribution separately, and permit no response. Ordinary textual role indicators may suffice. Never remove a consequential warning merely because a machine has made an offer or because an operator is silent. No operational command, real authority transfer, participant trial or efficacy claim is authorized here.

A negative result would be useful: if the instrument's handoff is musically dull, if labels are distracting, or if people cannot distinguish policy from timbre, retain the score as an artwork and abandon the proposed operational inference.

## Coda (original fiction)

The organ donated three keys to the visitors. The first was taken. The second was declined. The third was left on the floor.

At closing time the keeper counted three donations. The organist counted one new musician. The caretaker counted a key on the floor.

The curator asked the organ to accompany the silence.

It played nothing, as instructed. The curator did not record agreement.
