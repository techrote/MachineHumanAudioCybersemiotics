# A painting with four verbs

Original score, 25 November 2026. One or two performers, any quiet pitched instrument or sampler, optional reader. No audience participation required. The written score is not a reconstruction of any cited composition.

The field is four rows of semitone offsets from A3:

    0  2  4  7
    9  7  4  2
    0  5  9 12
    7  5  2  0

Each tile is a distinct position even if pitches recur. Use one quarter-second per tile, one fixed envelope, and the same timbre for all movements. The original recordings are simple additive realizations, not a claim that this is the best musical interpretation.

**I. Enumerate.** Read every row left to right, top to bottom. No additional gesture at the row-end leaps. Stop after sixteen tiles.

**II. Walk.** Read the top row left to right, the second right to left, and alternate until the bottom row is exhausted. Each consecutive event is a spatial neighbour. Do not introduce an extra pause at the turns.

**III. Turn the reading apparatus.** Use the transpose of the second route (down the first column, up the second, and so on). The painting is not transposed; only the route is. The pitch inventory remains identical.

**IV. The second verb.** Perform II followed by III, treating the boundary as a fresh pass. Their union exposes all 24 spatial edges as successive events, six twice. The optional reader may name the edges absent from II during III, or remain silent. The supplied two_pass recording is the unannotated version.

**Optional coda — The seam refuses to explain itself.** Choose a single omitted spatial edge from II. Sound both endpoints as a simultaneous dyad, or choose a nonlocal raster jump and prolong its two notes instead. These are different gestures. No need to complete a comprehensive picture. End.

## Fiction: the curator's order

The museum received a painting with instructions to play it. Its conservator found sixteen squares and no first square. She hired a reader who walked the top row, jumped to the other side of the next row, and repeated the procedure. A second reader walked without jumping. The conservator entered both readings under the same inventory number.

A visitor asked which sound represented the painting's left side. The conservator asked whether the visitor wanted to hear the left side, or to hear the way the first reader arrived there.

At the next exhibition the museum displayed the route next to the painting. In the corner of the route was a tiny line returning nowhere. It was labelled: THIS IS NOT A PART OF THE PAINTING. The composer played it anyway.
