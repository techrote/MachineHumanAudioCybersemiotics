# Four Ways to Cross the Same Room
Original text score, 2026-11-24. For one navigator, one weather composer, and an optional percussionist. No performance or listening data claimed.

## Materials and agreement

Four contrasting sounds A, B, C, D, arranged on a ring in that order. Suggestions: four quiet pitched tones at 220, 275, 330 and 385 Hz, or four accessible sound sources agreed by the performers. A route always goes to an adjacent sound. The weather composer specifies a list of four clockwise costs, each an integer from 0 to 8; reversing an edge costs eight minus its clockwise cost.

The cost is a score mark. No physical load, breath-holding, constrained posture, or repeated manual effort is required. Each musician can use any input modality, omit a gesture, ask another player to operate a control, or stop. All volumes and tempi are mutually comfortable. Silence is a valid contribution. The optional percussionist may instead say the number, show a card, or omit the cost accompaniment altogether.

Fixed-time realization: at the beginning of each cell (four comfortable beats), sound the current vertex, then choose the next vertex. During the rest of the cell the percussionist may express the traversed edge's cost as that many light pulses spaced within the remaining beats, or display the numeral. At the next cell sound the destination. The principal voice never changes its duration merely because a cost changes. A separate elastic-time realization may use additional beats for the cost, at everyone's discretion; label that realization differently.

## I. The map, before the weather

Set clockwise costs (4,4,4,4). Perform A B C D A, then A D C B A. Each journey pays sixteen counters. Both arrive at A. Hear the endpoint separately from the path; do not try to make the two different intervening melodies sound identical.

## II. The hills

Set clockwise costs (5,5,5,1); reverse costs (3,3,3,7) in paired edge order. Perform A B C and then A D C. Both pay ten counters. The weather composer privately chooses the second route. The percussionist may reveal the edge costs but must not pretend that these imply a particular human sensation. Next compare the full circuits in each direction; each pays sixteen.

## III. Prevailing wind

Set clockwise costs (1,1,1,1), reverse costs (7,7,7,7). Repeat the exact principal sequence of movement I, using the same durations and timbres. The clockwise journey now pays four and the anticlockwise journey twenty-eight. Only the cost accompaniment changes relative to movement I. Let the navigator improvise eight more adjacent transitions with the costs visible, then repeat that exact improvised sequence under the opposite wind (7,7,7,7). Do not infer willingness, ability or musical preference from the chosen route.

Optional duo: a second navigator starts at A with the opposing wind. Both choose paths independently. When both arrive at A, they may stop or exchange weather scores; no winner is announced.

## IV. The wind that is refused

The navigator may turn the cost voice off, replace the field by level terrain, or keep the existing wind. The weather composer continues to write fields on paper regardless, but has no authority to impose them. Perform any four adjacent transitions, or end without another sound. The written weather remains a separate artifact.

## Fiction: A survey of the interior coastline

The instrument maker was commissioned to reproduce a musical room whose doors all opened. She took careful measurements of the rooms beyond each door and of the notes they contained. The replica was judged perfect.

A musician who had known the original went around the replica twice. She asked where the draft had gone. The maker replied that there was no missing room.

In the original, the musician had sometimes walked the wrong way round merely to resist the draft. She had found an entire evening of music in that decision. The maker proposed a stronger spring for every door.

The musician declined. She asked for a wind that could be switched off.

Years later the museum displayed two maps of the same building. The first was a floor plan. The second had arrows. The emergency exit was not included in the arrows; it remained an exit in all weather.
