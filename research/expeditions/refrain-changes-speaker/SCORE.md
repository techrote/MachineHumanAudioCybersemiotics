# A bell on both sides of the glass
Original text score and speculative afterword. For two performers, an optional third, one sampler and an imaginary counter. Nothing is connected to machinery. All pitches are original arbitrary choices, not recognized warning tones.

## I. The object
Play eight notes: 220, 330, 275, 440, 220, 275, 330, 440 Hz. Equal duration. Treat the first two as operative for a toy counter, the remaining six as exhibits. Repeat the exact sequence with the last two operative instead. Do not change the sounds. Reveal the two counter timelines afterwards. If the counter has no perceptible effect, the two performances are acoustically identical; that is the point.

## II. The quotation inside the quotation
A declares QUOTE. B places LIVE inside it. A places REHEARSE inside that. B plays one note. The counter remains still. A displays the entire chain. B may then make a separate LIVE note referring to the displayed object; the counter advances once. Neither performer claims to have restored the old note's historical power. They have made a new event.

## III. The late caption
A performs the sequence again. B must choose an operative or exhibition footer only after the last note. C, if present, records which events would have incremented the counter under an eager policy and which did under the delayed one. Try a version with the label at the beginning. Discuss the differences without asking anyone to infer an unheard label.

## IV. The curator's counterpoint
The players are now free to put the counter's increments into a quiet second sound layer. The audible object becomes a melodic ground; the otherwise silent history of its disposition becomes an accompaniment. Exchange roles. End with the counter audible but the original motif absent. Do not announce the ending until the silence has been heard.

## Afterword — The municipal bell

The bell had a certificate stating that it could close the gates. The museum acquired it together with the certificate. They installed the certificate in the frame above the bell and called the exhibit complete.

The first visitor was a former gatekeeper. She asked whether the bell still closed the gates.

The curator said it sounded exactly as it used to.

She asked the question again.

Eventually they invited the town's last machinist to look at the wiring. He traced the wire from the bell to the amplifier and from the amplifier to a pair of loudspeakers. Then he examined the certificate.

The curator proposed replacing the certificate with a sign explaining that the bell no longer closed anything.

The machinist objected. It had once closed things. That was the reason to keep the certificate.

They hung a second sign.

In the following years the two signs acquired separate histories. The first became progressively more difficult to read. The second was periodically renewed. Children learned the song of the bell from the speakers, and played it on their own instruments, in the street, among the houses. There were no gates left.

One winter the town installed a new gate. The engineer used a different sound for its closing, but the gatekeeper's daughter complained that the new signal reminded her of the museum.

The engineer offered to alter the signal.

She declined. She asked him instead to put the museum's two signs on the wall beside the gate.

He made three.

The third was dated that morning.
