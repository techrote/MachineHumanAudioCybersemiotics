# Score for an Exhaustion Organ

Original text score and speculative fiction; no performed or recorded realization claimed.

## Movement I — Two syllables, eight obligations
Choose two comfortable sounds, A and B. Begin with two silent setup symbols AA. Each played symbol completes a three-symbol phrase. Place a token on the corresponding phrase in a table of eight. Never place two tokens on the same phrase. Play ABAA (A=0), then stop. Do not fill the empty table cells by starting from an unearned context. Listen to the rest that follows. The listener may ask for a repeat, but the performer may only point at the rule.

## Movement II — The right to finish
Reset visibly. Play ABABBBAA from the same setup. This time all eight cells fill. Play the setup as sound only if you announce that it is setup and not counted. Invite a second performer to read the complete table in reverse order while the first repeats the original score. Neither is obliged to interpret the other's reading as music.

## Movement III — A disagreement of scale
While the first performer repeats Movement II, a second counts one-symbol recurrence, a third counts two-symbol recurrence, and a fourth counts three-symbol recurrence. Each rings a small bell when a recurrence occurs at their scale. The first traversal starts with a declared cyclic convention: count eight windows, including the wraparound ones, exactly once. Repeat with an open-ended history and no resets. Compare the bell rhythms. Do not vote on which listener is right.

## Movement IV — The permission to remember
The listener may now request any earlier phrase. The performer may answer exactly, vary it, or remain silent. Every answer is outside the anti-repeat constraint, not an exception secretly inserted into it. The performance ends when a listener requests something that nobody remembers. This is a human performance instruction, not a measurable guarantee.

## Fiction: The Bureau of Unprecedented Sounds

The Bureau bought a machine that could not repeat itself. At first the machinery was smaller than the certificate, and the certificate was kept in a separate room.

Its inventor had defined a sound as five consecutive notes. The clerk understood a sound as a single note. The music teacher understood a sound as something a child had not yet learned to recognize. At the public demonstration all three kept impeccable records and all three disagreed.

They eventually built a second machine which prevented the first from making sounds that would prevent it from making other sounds. The Bureau displayed two counters, one for unused possibilities and one for reachable possibilities. Citizens asked why the counters differed. The engineer drew a map of the hall, with a door that locked behind anyone who used it.

A child asked the instrument to play the tune from the beginning. The instrument could not. The engineer offered to erase its ledger. The child refused: the pleasure was not in making the tune new again.

At the next exhibition the Bureau added a button labelled AGAIN. It did not reset the ledger. For the first time the instrument was allowed to remember what it was repeating.
