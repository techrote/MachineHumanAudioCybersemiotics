# The conductor who was not there
*Original speculative fiction. No empirical or historical claims.*

The first conductor had a baton. The second conductor had a switch. The third was a theorem written in the margin of a score.

The two players sat in soundproof cubicles. There was a clock, but no stage. The composer sent each of them a small strip of paper with a hole punched in its end. A hole meant play. A strip with two holes meant that the other had received the first. A strip with three holes meant that the recipient of two holes had received the receipt.

The audience asked whether they would have to hear the entire paper machine. The composer said no. The music lasted two seconds.

At the beat the players struck the same note. A recording engineer drew two parallel lines on a monitor and announced that the experiment had succeeded.

In the next room the composer replaced every strip with a single lamp visible to both performers. They struck the note again. The engineer, who had not seen the substitution, wrote that the experiments were identical.

A critic protested. The first duet had performed its uncertainty, even though none of it was in the sound. The second had performed a shared warrant.

A technician protested in turn. The word 'shared' was not permitted unless both players knew the lamp was visible to the other. She removed a small panel from one wall. It was a mirror. Behind it was another switch.

At the third rehearsal there was a note in the left channel and silence in the right. The audience finally heard a difference, and immediately invented a quarrel between the performers. The right-hand player had never received the invitation.

The composer revised the programme notes: the work had four movements, three of which could be heard.

For the fourth movement, both players were asked to play only if they could be certain that the other would play under every allowed failure of the paper machine. They inspected the score. They waited. The sound engineer discovered that the recorder was working perfectly.

At last the critic wrote a review of the silence. It contained the word 'trust' eleven times. Neither performer recognized the description.

The fourth movement was not about the absence of trust. It was about a guarantee whose price was a rest.

Years later a museum bought the recording. It accessioned both identical versions under the same catalogue number, and separately displayed the paper strips and the lamp. Children were allowed to play with the switches.

It was the children who discovered the work could be performed in reverse. Not by reversing the recording, but by beginning with a shared instruction and gradually making the means of knowing it private.

The sound did not change until the very end. This time, when the solo note appeared, nobody blamed the other player.
