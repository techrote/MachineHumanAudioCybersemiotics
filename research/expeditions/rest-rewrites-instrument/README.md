## 1. Essay: the difference between not sounding and not happening

A keyboard with one button sits under a glass dome. Pressing the button sounds a note; releasing it silences the machine. The manual says that the instrument has five sounds, but contains no pitch control. Its pitch selector advances one position for each complete unit of time spent released. While sounding, its selector is held.

The silence is not an absence of composition. It is the only place where pitch can be composed.

There is a seductive but misleading image of the digital instrument as a collection of controls that await the performer. This image identifies agency with overt intervention: a person turns a dial and the machine changes. It omits the electronic and computational history that takes place between interventions. An LFO advances, a delay buffer empties, a physical material cools, a sensor baseline adapts. Even the decision to arrest such processes during a pause is an intervention by the designer.

The familiar note/rest distinction therefore conceals several independently adjustable dimensions:

- acoustic emission (sound or no sound);
- process evolution (hold, advance, relax, reset, environmental input);
- agency (explicit gesture, deliberate withholding, unintentional absence, other processes);
- knowledge (what the performer can inspect or anticipate when returning).

None logically determines the others. A muted oscillator can remain active. A stopped process can leave a resonating body audible. A resting human can have a highly active perceptual relationship to a sound. A silent clock can change the affordances of the next gesture without producing an intermediate sound.

This also suggests a counterargument to the ideal of the self-playing instrument. The interesting possibility here is not a machine filling silence with new sonic events. It is one that preserves the silence while transforming the conditions of future sonic events. Its contribution occurs in the conditional, not in the present waveform.

## 2. Source-located encounters (not a systematic review)

**Cage / receptivity.** David Platzker, “Silence Is Not What It Used to Be,” MoMA, 28 October 2013, [article](https://www.moma.org/explore/inside_out/2013/10/28/silence-is-not-what-it-used-to-be-2/), paragraphs beginning “The title of the exhibition” through the two paragraphs of Cage's April 1954 letter to Helen Wolff. Read the online passage and curator context; did not independently collate the manuscript. Cage describes his piece in terms of unplanned sounds encountered during its performance, and differences in listeners' receptivity. MoMA's [exhibition description](https://www.moma.org/calendar/exhibitions/1386) (paragraph beginning “On a warm summer evening”) describes the three movements and absence of deliberate performer sound. The present experiment is not an explanation or recreation of 4′33″. Its gap rule is explicitly deterministic and its silent computational activity not ordinarily audible. The contrast is productive: a composer can open the environment to hearing, or close the output while opening the instrument to internal change.

**Pask / adaptation.** Jon Bird and Ezequiel Di Paolo, “Gordon Pask and His Maverick Machines” (2008), [DOI](https://doi.org/10.7551/mitpress/9780262083775.003.0008), [accessible chapter transcription](https://www.academia.edu/54223516/Gordon_Pask_His_Maverick_Machines), printed pp. 190–193, sections “Musicolour,” “How Does Musicolour Work?” and “Was It a Success?” Read those passages, not Pask's cited 1962/1971 originals. The authors describe adaptive filter selection in the sound-driven light system, habituation during repeated input, and increased environmental sensitivity when input is absent. The mapping developed through interaction. This is an especially relevant historical alternative to a static controller. It is not evidence that adaptation is always wanted, that the machine experiences boredom, or that Pask's mechanisms are implemented here. In fact the chapter's account of the less successful large ballroom installation (pp. 194–195, paragraph beginning “After a year”) is a useful limit: an interaction can fail because the surrounding practice changes.

**Tudor / material mediation.** The Kitchen archive, [Rainforest, April 19, 1975](https://thekitchen.org/on-file/rainforest/), two descriptive paragraphs under the title; read the text, not a performance recording. The archive describes excited physical objects, feedback and collective construction of electronic/object systems, and distinguishes the later automated Rainforest V. The [Getty catalogue](https://www.getty.edu/research/tools/guides_bibliographies/david_tudor/av/rainforest.html), “Rainforest IV (1973, realized 2001),” corroborates the installation context and individual construction of resonant objects. The material and the venue are not transparent carriers. This is a counterweight to our entirely virtual selector: an actual resonator may keep sounding after a human's intervention stops. Our silence is a declared digital gate, not a physical guarantee.

**Simondon / milieu.** Susanna Lindberg, “Being with Technique–Technique as being-with: The technological communities of Gilbert Simondon,” Continental Philosophy Review 52 (2019), 299–310, [open text](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11007-019-09466-9), DOI 10.1007/s11007-019-09466-9. Read numbered portions (1) and the associated-milieu passage in (2), around notes 35–41, and opening paragraphs of (3). Lindberg explains how a technical individual and its enabling surroundings are interrelated, and develops an interpretation of technical objects through individuation. Her piano example is her own exposition, not Simondon's original example. Only her article was consulted; no primary-text reconstruction. A simple changing selector does not instantiate Simondon's ontology. It merely provides a concrete opportunity to question the choice of an instrument's boundaries: is the clock inside, outside, or shared with a performance environment?

**Synthesis (interpretation):** Cage's receptive frame, Pask's adaptive interaction, Tudor's materially mediated signal and Lindberg's reading of technical milieu are not interchangeable. They disagree in precisely the useful ways: unplanned sound versus programmed response; silence as context versus silence as latent control; stable rules versus evolving rules. No inference about human perception or Brier's endorsement follows from placing them together.

## 3. Finite model: duration as control

Fix m >= 2 distinct pitch labels, initial selector 0, n >= 1 identical attacks, and T nonnegative integer silent ticks allocated to the n pre-attack gaps. Each attack has the same fixed duration and does not advance the selector. Gap g_i advances the selector by g_i modulo m; the attack reads its position. There is no trailing gap. Zero ticks still permit distinct consecutive attacks. The observed pitch-only score is p_i = (g_1+...+g_i) mod m. Every score ends on T mod m; its duration and gesture count are identical. Timing is deliberately excluded from the pitch-only count; full audio also distinguishes some gap redistributions.

The residue vector r_i = g_i mod m is uniquely recoverable from the pitch path using successive modular differences (with p_0=0). A vector with each r_i in 0..m-1 is realizable exactly when its sum S <= T and S is congruent to T modulo m: necessity follows from g_i=r_i+m k_i, sufficiency by assigning all remaining multiples of m to the first gap. This gives the exact capacity:

C(m,n,T) = sum of [z^s](1+z+...+z^(m-1))^n over 0 <= s <= T with s congruent to T mod m.

There are m^(n-1) candidate pitch paths ending at the required endpoint, because the first n-1 pitches may be arbitrary. The largest possible residue sum congruent to T is L = n(m-1) - ((n(m-1)-T) mod m), with modulo nonnegative. This sum exists: all integers from 0 through n(m-1) are realizable by n bounded digits. Thus saturation occurs exactly when T >= L. In particular every T >= (n-1)(m-1) saturates, since T+m > n(m-1). For n>1 the immediately preceding T=(n-1)(m-1)-1 fails because the all-(m-1) vector needs T+m ticks. For n=1 the uniform threshold is zero. This establishes the sharp eventual threshold, without claiming a new combinatorial theorem.

For m=5,n=4: T=0,4,8,11,12 yields 1,35,105,124,125 distinct pitch paths respectively. At T=12 the endpoint is label 2. Compare allocations (12,0,0,0) yielding (2,2,2,2), (3,3,3,3) yielding (3,1,4,2), and (0,4,4,4) yielding (0,4,3,2). All share the same total silence and four equal attacks. The fourth path in the score contrasts an event-clock HOLD policy, which would produce (0,0,0,0) at any T.

The independent enumeration in lab.js (run node lab.js) checks 365 configurations, 374,648 weak compositions, 130,923 candidate residue vectors and 506,845 assertions. It uses small exact integer arithmetic, direct enumeration of gap allocations, a bounded-digit polynomial recurrence and a separate residue witness test. It verifies the endpoint, capacity and sharp threshold. For larger parameters use BigInt or another exact arithmetic implementation. No real listener participated; equality of pitch labels is stipulated and not measured discriminability.

## 4. Design hypothesis: the Rest Bus

An experimental synth should expose four independently routable time domains: transport time; time elapsed while a gate is open; time elapsed while the gate is closed; and explicitly stepped time. An LFO, matrix permutation, wavetable reader, envelope topology or tuning rule can be driven by any of them. An evolving process may publish its state without sounding. A rest can be rehearsed against a shadow clock before it is committed. Distinguish mute (emission), freeze (evolution), reset (state), and pause (transport); none should silently substitute for the others.

A performance-oriented version could let silence accumulate preparation credits for a patch, changing the available routing at the next attack. A second player could choose whether to advance or hold during silence, while the first controls only attacks. In contrast to an always-generating sequencer, the instrument never sounds without an explicit attack. A patch could store an idle-policy contract, with deterministic time units, state snapshots and explicit resumption rules. A performer may disable evolution or inspect a predicted return; deliberate uncertainty must be an elective artistic feature, not an access barrier.

A machine notification interface is a different use case. A silent interval may contain baseline recalibration, queued work, an unresolved obligation, an input failure or a frozen process. A resume announcement could disclose relevant changes since the last acknowledged state instead of implying that silence meant stability. Proposed test: randomized synthetic histories with equal initial and final values and equal idle duration but different intermediate state changes; compare a bare resumption display against version and state-transition summaries, measuring correct discrimination and workload. Include unchanged histories and false-alarm cost. This is a design hypothesis only; do not change the canonical protocol or present silent-state evolution as an empirical finding. Avoid using unannounced semantic drift in operational signals. No device control, recruitment, safety policy or production implementation is authorized.

## 5. What would make the project wrong?

A performer may experience every changed post-rest mapping as a failure of reliability, not a resource. A screen showing every hidden transition may be intrusive. Physical time is not a perfect discrete clock; real instrument evolution may depend on temperature, pressure, other musicians and the activity during attacks. A clock tick here means exactly one declared unit of idle time, not an inference of intent or awareness. A machine cannot infer why a person waited from duration alone. A stochastic or adaptive process may yield very different capacity and predictability. In a real instrument, timing itself is audible, and the present counting result is not a compression theorem about waveforms.

The artistic claim survives these limitations in a narrow form: compose not only the sounded event, but the law governing what the instrument becomes during the absence of that event. That law can be held constant, performed, or refused. The accompanying score is an original proposal, not a human performance report.
