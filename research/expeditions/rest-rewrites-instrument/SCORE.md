# Twelve Units of Absence

Original text score, 24 November 2026. For one key, five pitches, a silent clock and optionally a second performer. Not a realization of Cage or Tudor. No performance or listening data are claimed.

## Apparatus

Choose five clearly labeled pitches (suggested 220, 247, 294, 330, 392 Hz). Each attack sounds for 200 ms; each silent tick lasts 300 ms. The selector starts at pitch index zero. A closed gate advances the selector by one per silent tick; an open gate holds it. A zero-tick gap means consecutive articulated attacks, not overlapping ones. Perform with a metered clock or render the discrete score. Do not treat a user's unintended absence as consent to change an instrument.

## Movement I — Three equivalent durations

Perform four attacks with each of the following gap allocations. Reset the selector between performances, outside the scored time. Do not move a pitch control.

| Allocation | Pitch index path | Total silence |
|---|---|---|
| 12,0,0,0 | 2,2,2,2 | 12 |
| 3,3,3,3 | 3,1,4,2 | 12 |
| 0,4,4,4 | 0,4,3,2 | 12 |

Each performance lasts 4.4 seconds. These are distinct pitch paths, not necessarily equally salient to listeners. In the second movement the same clock becomes another performer's instrument.

## Movement II — The clock player's veto

Repeat the middle allocation. A second performer selects ADVANCE or HOLD for each silent tick. Neither may alter any note duration, and neither may add an attack. Display the selected policy on a separate card or screen. The first performer may propose a different allocation, but must retain the total of twelve ticks. Alternate the roles. The score ends when both performers agree to repeat a realization without changing it, or when they choose to stop.

## Movement III — The frozen museum

Perform the same gap allocations in HOLD mode. All notes have index zero. The pitch sequence is invariant, the rhythms are not. Now perform ADVANCE again, but keep the display hidden by mutual agreement. Finally reveal the state and repeat the first allocation. Discuss what was difficult to anticipate, rather than declaring one policy more musical.

## Movement IV — A score that does not require sound

Sit beside the released instrument. Let the clock run for any agreed interval. Do not press the key. At the end, either disclose the selector or leave it unobserved. The score has an internal state even if no waveform was produced. In a room there may be other sounds. No claim of acoustic silence is made.

## Speculative fiction: the instrument left in a room

The museum had two acquisitions described by their makers as the same instrument. One was connected to a clock that stopped when the gallery closed. The second was connected to the clock above the station. Their keys and sound libraries were identical.

When the conservator arrived each morning, she pressed the first key in each machine. The first returned to the note she had expected. The second returned to a note that had been prepared by the night.

She attempted to conserve the instruments by disconnecting both clocks. The acquisition committee praised her for restoring their equivalence. The composer of the second instrument objected: the night had been removed.

The committee eventually decided that it would preserve the clock relationship as part of the work, alongside the circuit and the source code. A visitor could ask for the clock to be stopped, but the attendant would record the request.

On the final day of the exhibition there was a strike. Nobody entered the museum. No instrument was played. The machine connected to the station continued to count the hours.

The following morning the conservator noticed that the label beside it described a work that had not been performed the day before. She rewrote the label. She did not know whether the clock had performed the work. She knew only that the first note was no longer the one she would have heard yesterday.
