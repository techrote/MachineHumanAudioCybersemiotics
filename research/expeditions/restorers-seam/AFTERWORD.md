# The Conservator of the Interruption

*Original speculative fiction; not historical, philosophical or empirical evidence.*

The oldest instrument in the city was not a violin. It was a collection of recordings made by a device no one could reconstruct, preserved in a building whose ventilation made a more convincing imitation of the music than the modern restorations did.

Three notes were missing from its best-known piece. The first and last were the same, and so the machine employed by the museum supplied three more of them. There was a certain economy to this. It was the least startling solution, which the committee took as a mark of the master's restraint.

A junior conservator entered the missing region in the catalogue as a separate object. She called it the Interruption. Its dimensions were known. Its contents were not.

At the opening she played the familiar version, then another, in which the three absent notes rose and fell like a faulty elevator. A member of the committee asked her to stop vandalising the archive.

She replied that the archive was unchanged. She could prove it with a checksum, which unfortunately sounded nothing like music.

Years later the museum commissioned a new organ. It had two manuals. On the first, a performer could play any reconstruction that agreed with the surviving fragments. The second produced only the notes the performer had supplied. Visitors could hear a plausible history in one ear and the present day in the other. Sometimes the two merged into a single exquisite chord. Sometimes the right speaker fell silent, and a genuine ancient fragment stood alone.

The curator eventually admitted that neither speaker was quite the past. The recording machine, the building, and even the decision about where a note ended had histories of their own. But the two-manual organ made one kind of ignorance honest and playable.

On the instrument's nameplate they engraved no date of invention. Instead, they engraved the dates of the things that had survived, the dates of those who had repaired them, and three empty spaces.

The spaces were the only part no one was permitted to restore.
