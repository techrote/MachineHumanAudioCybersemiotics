# Five Studies for a Broken Construction

Original text score, 2026-11-24. Two musicians, one adjudicator. Tones may be substituted for gestures. No claims about psychological effects. The supplied recordings are a small mechanical realization, not the entire score.

**I. The sentence without a past.** Leave four beats empty. Perform a four-note phrase in the left voice. The other voice does nothing.

**II. The sentence with an abandoned beginning.** Perform two notes. Do not complete them. After two rests, play the same four-note phrase as before. Do not announce whether it continues, replaces or ignores the first two notes.

**III. The editorial instrument.** Repeat II, but introduce a separate audible mark after the abandoned fragment. Before the performance, disclose that the mark designates a replacement. Observe that the mark cannot itself erase the old sounds.

**IV. Revision counterpoint.** As the final phrase returns, transfer the abandoned two notes to the right voice. Repeat them twice. They are no longer the beginning of the left voice but remain part of the piece. Repeat with a different abandoned fragment and the same final left phrase.

**V. The refusal of the archive.** A performer abandons a fragment. The adjudicator asks whether to preserve it, transform it, or let it disappear. The performer may decline to decide. Silence remains permissible. No further sound is required.

## Fiction: The Ministry of Perfect Sentences

The city's first synthetic speaker was built with a device that removed every incomplete sentence before transmission. It was much praised for never having to change its mind.

In the listening archive, an engineer discovered that every speaker had been issued two clocks. One measured the time between beginning and ending a sentence. The other measured the time between beginning and abandoning one. The second clock had never been connected to a display.

She attached it to an oscillator.

The next broadcast contained a phrase that was never allowed to complete. The ministry asked her to correct it. She corrected it, then played the discarded construction on a different channel.

A committee asked which of the two voices belonged to the sentence. She could answer that precisely: the left voice belonged to the sentence; the right voice belonged to the occasion on which it had been rewritten.

The committee ordered the right voice to be preserved.

At the next broadcast the engineer disconnected it. She said that the right to revise was not the obligation to exhibit every revision.
