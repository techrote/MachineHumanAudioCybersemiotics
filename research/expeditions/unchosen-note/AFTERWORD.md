# The Museum of Unplayed Notes

*Original speculative score and fiction. No historical or empirical claim.*

In the museum, instruments were catalogued by the notes they had made. There were three copper organs in the east wing. Each had performed every approved song exactly once. The curator therefore listed them as interchangeable.

The first organ had three keys. The second had three keys, of which two were locked by a device inside the case. Every morning the locks changed. The third had two available keys at any given moment, but no one key remained available throughout the week.

All three were capable of the same catalogue.

A conservator proposed that their differences be documented by recording the sounds of the keys that could not be pressed. The archivist explained that silence could not be assigned a catalogue number: there was no recording, only an unsuccessful event. The conservator made a second proposal. Preserve a little map of the instrument at each moment in a performance, including all the doors that were open but not entered.

The archive refused. Such maps described songs that had never existed.

Years later, the organs were restored. Their tones were copied meticulously into identical digital replicas. Every sound survived. The second organ's restrictions were omitted because they made no difference to the collection. The third's changing permissions were omitted because no one could agree which settings were original. The first organ's freedom was preserved accidentally, because giving the user all the samples was the cheapest implementation.

At the opening ceremony, the curator announced the resurrection of three identical instruments. A machinist asked whether an instrument could be faithfully restored by changing when the musician was allowed to decide. The curator replied that the restoration was perfectly faithful to the recordings.

The question was left unanswered in the catalogue.

## Performance instruction — three windows

Perform the same A–B phrase on three instruments. For the first, keep B, C and D available until the ending. For the second, select B before playing A and lock the remaining options. For the third, leave only B and C available. Do not make any sound indicating the restrictions during the phrase.

Then perform a second pass in which each momentary menu is visible. Allow the audience to interrupt and request an ending. Document refusals. Do not secretly change the menu in response to a request.

For the third pass, make the availability of an alternative itself a compositional event: preserve an unused key for as long as possible, then remove it at a boundary chosen by the performer. No particular musical effect is promised. The score's material is the difference between hearing a note, being allowed to play it, and keeping it unplayed.

The three passes are not a human-subject experiment; they are an original artwork instruction. Any later empirical reception study requires separate design and approval.
