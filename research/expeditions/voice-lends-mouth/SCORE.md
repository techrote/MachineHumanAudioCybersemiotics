# A Mouth Is Not a Signature
## Original score for two electronic performers and a clerk, with optional observer

Use four distinguishable pitches, 220, 330, 275, 385 Hz (or freely transposed). Each note occupies one quarter-second slot, with a short fade and silence before the next. The four slots are followed by four equally long base tones at 165 Hz. A countervoice, when requested, sounds 770, 550, 660, 440 Hz in those final four slots. All of these pitches are original stipulated material for this exercise, not borrowed from any recording.

### I. The mouth

Play the eight slots with no countervoice. Let the clerk mark the original as replayed, with its parent reference. Repeat exactly, this time marking it as cited. The listener may not inspect the marks until both performances have finished. No difference is required in their hearing. The clerk has changed the description of a relationship, not the pitches.

### II. The guest conductor

Repeat the first four notes exactly. The second performer now adopts the four pitches as a rule: reverse them and double their frequencies, playing the resulting sequence over the base tone. Repeat with the citation declaration switched on. Do not change the notes. If the observer asks which of the two second-half performances cited its source, disclose that the score withholds acoustic evidence of that declaration.

### III. The unwelcome inheritance

Pass the motif down a line of four musicians, each marking whether they adopt the transformation locally. First use the marks exactly. Then try the alternative rule: if an earlier player adopted the countervoice, every later player must sound it. No participant is required to enact an unwanted rule; the clerk can represent this alternative entirely on cards. Compare the two sequences. Write a third, deliberately chosen inheritance rule. No claim that one policy is always aesthetically superior.

### IV. The unsounded source

The guest performer may ask to contribute a pattern but decline playback of its original form. The other musician may propose a new transformation, ask permission, decline, or end the piece. If they proceed, only the new pattern need sound. This movement is intentionally not implemented by the finite renderer; its negotiated relationships are part of the performance.

### Afterword: The municipal mouth

The town kept the voices of its former bell makers in a narrow cabinet. On the cabinet was a button that would pronounce one old instruction in a perfectly reconstructed voice.

A new keeper pressed it every morning. The door of the workshop opened.

One morning she was showing the cabinet to a visitor. The door opened again, although the keeper had only wanted to exhibit the old voice.

She asked for another button. The engineer added a second speaker. Both buttons still opened the door.

The keeper removed the door wire and asked the engineer to return it as a separate cable. In the next concert she connected it not to a door, but to a little bank of oscillators. The old voice could govern the oscillators if she wished. When she disconnected them, the voice was still there.

The archive was not made innocent by this alteration. It was made capable of being listened to without being obeyed.

*Fiction and artistic proposition, not an operational or social model. No actual historical voice is used.*
