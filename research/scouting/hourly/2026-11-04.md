### PARALLEL — action-relevant modality comparisons, not cue-family rankings

**P39 / S133 — multimodal alert may help when a response is urgent.** Ho, Reed & Spence (2007) compared auditory-only, tactile-only and audiotactile warnings in a collision-avoidance driving simulator, including background-radio and brake-light manipulations. The **primary publisher abstract** reports faster *brake initiation* for combined cues than either single channel. **Class:** reported within-experiment human behavioral result; **analogy** to MHAC. **Connection:** R013/R014 and #30 distinguish response initiation from semantic comprehension, correct authorized action, workload and workload cost. **Value:** high, because the study uses a consequential action proxy rather than only sound recognition. **Confidence:** high identity, moderate abstract-level result, low transfer. **Next:** R005 eligibility/identity check, R009 outcome taxonomy; possible R014 conditional matched-content/latency design. **Countertest:** redundant cue speeds first movement but increases wrong action or fails deadline-correct action in a new domain. No real collision-safety conclusion for MHAC.

**P40 / S134 — tactile-only can outperform other warning implementations under some timing conditions.** Scott & Gray (2008) studied 16 simulator participants under no-warning, auditory, visual and tactile rear-end collision conditions at two time-to-collision thresholds (3 and 5 s). The publisher abstract reports the *shortest mean* brake response time for tactile warnings and statistically shorter times than **no-warning and visual** conditions; do **not** claim a statistically significant tactile-versus-auditory superiority from this abstract. **Class:** small primary simulator result and **counterexample** to assuming audio is always fastest. **Connection:** R008's non-audio alternative framework, R013/R014 comparative baselines, and S127/S131 on varying reception/tolerability. **Value:** high as a negative-control modality. **Confidence:** high identity, moderate abstract result, low population/setting transfer. **Next:** candidate corpus / R005 cutoff and R006 identity; retain explicit tactile alternative without expanding MHAC into a haptic product.

### ORTHOGONAL — mechanism and statistical discipline

**O37 / S135 — faster redundant responses do not by themselves show integrated information or better understanding.** Gondan & Minakata (2016) provide an expert **methodological tutorial** on redundant-signals race-model-inequality (RMI) tests, highlighting false-positive rate, statistical power, omissions, anticipations and interpretation. A simple fastest-channel race can speed response even without integrated semantic processing. **Class:** methodological tool, not MHAC-specific experimental evidence. **Connection:** challenge strong claims about multimodal synergy in R008/R009; distinguish reaction-time distribution, action error, and comprehension. **Value:** high for avoiding false mechanism attribution. **Confidence:** high for abstract/tutorial metadata, moderate for method implications pending full-text appraisal. **Next:** use an RMI methodological checklist only if real RT distributions are eventually available under authorized R020; a synthetic timing model is only a rehearsal, never a substitute for participants. The already scouted S44's RMI application is a **reuse**, not a new record.

**O38 / S136 — cue-driven action and perceptual attention can diverge.** Ho, Tan & Spence (2006) used spatially predictive auditory and vibrotactile cues under high visual-attention load and an orthogonal cue/task arrangement. In the publisher abstract, **valid auditory** cues facilitated later visual-target discrimination; equally informative tactile cues did **not** in that arrangement, despite earlier cue studies showing improved driving responses. The authors distinguish attentional cuing from motor-response priming and propose a spatial-co-location explanation. **Class:** primary empirical within-task result; alternative explanatory mechanism; setting-limited. **Connection:** #30's causal failure stages and R008's simpler perceptual/response accounts can explain some action improvement without assigning additional semantic meaning to cues. **Value:** high; **confidence:** high identity, moderate abstract effect, low transfer. **Next:** R009 outcome extraction candidate, R013/R014 contrasting spatial cue validity and response mapping. Not evidence tactile warning systems generally fail.

### PERPENDICULAR — extra channels and valid cues may impose costs

**X41 / S137 — redundant warnings' apparent benefit depends on workload and can carry affective cost.** Biondi, Leo, Gastaldi, Rossi & Mulatti (2017) compared auditory, tactile and combined in-vehicle warnings under workload/interpulse manipulations. The primary publisher abstract reports that the **low-workload** multimodal benefit was not seen as overall workload rose; it explicitly evaluated urgency, annoyance and acceptability. **Class:** reported source result and counterposition; no MHAC outcome validated. **Connection:** revisits S44/S45 without recounting them; #31 and R013/R014 should reject unconditional redundant-channel prescriptions. **Value:** high as workload falsifier; **confidence:** high identity, moderate within-task claim, low MHAC transfer. **Next:** R005/R006 candidate search, separate response benefit from annoyance/acceptance and time pressure. Avoid assuming more channels are benign or universally effective.

**X42 / S138 — the *timing* of a directional warning can reverse its apparent benefit.** Tilak, Xholi, Schowalter, Ferris, Hameed & Sarter (2008) manipulated valid/invalid auditory and tactile cues, stimulus-onset asynchrony and simulated driving workload. Their primary conference abstract reports that valid tactile spatial cues helped more at shorter (~100–200 ms) onsets, while auditory validity helped at 500 ms and was **slower at 1000 ms**. These are task-specific cue effects, not an intrinsic auditory latency law. **Class:** empirical counterevidence to monotonic lead-time or modality advantage. **Connection:** #30's timing and attention failure modes, #41's received-channel tests and R014's prospective timing controls. **Value:** high for a falsifiable counterexample, **confidence:** high identity, moderate abstract-level direction, low MHAC transfer. **Next:** compare cue lead, message age, channel congruence, and false spatial guidance separately; never transplant these milliseconds as system defaults.

Conversely, a tactile or visual alternative might outperform audio under restricted reception or access conditions; that does not make either modality universally accessible.

### PARALLEL — interruption scheduling must not be confused with message semantics

**P41 / S139 — interruptibility policies have a time-critical exception.** McFarlane (2002) reports a **36-person experiment** comparing four coordination methods in an abstract multitask setting. The publisher abstract describes negotiated interruptions as best overall while immediate interruptions were preferable when small differences in timeliness mattered. **Class:** source-reported within-task empirical result; plausible *analogy*, not an acoustic effectiveness claim. **MHAC connection:** R012/R014, #33/#36: a low-interruption policy can conflict with a correct-action deadline even if every message is ultimately delivered. **Value:** high; **confidence:** high bibliographic identity / moderate abstract-level result / low transfer. **Action:** retain as a candidate comparator for R008/R009 and a boundary condition in R014; do not prescribe automatic negotiation. Note the 2002 print volume versus 2009 publisher webpage date, not a second study.

**P42 / S140 — partial metadata about a pending interruption may protect the primary task without proving faster secondary-task completion.** Ho, Nikolic, Waters & Sarter (2004) tested 48 people performing an ATC simulation. The NLM abstract says automatic information about pending-task priority and modality improved *ATC primary-task performance*, while experimental groups **did not significantly differ in interruption-task performance**. **Class:** source-reported empirical result and partial **counterevidence** to equating a scheduling aide with better warning response. **MHAC connection:** R013/R014 and #30 distinguish communicated priority, recipient choice, primary-task interference, completed action and deadline. **Value:** high; **confidence:** high identity / moderate within-task abstract / low MHAC transfer. **Action:** candidate corpus, separately code primary and interruption-task outcomes if formally eligible.

### ORTHOGONAL — task-boundary workload and the data-age clock are different metrics

**O39 / S141 — lower-workload task boundaries are a measurable candidate, not a universal safe-delivery moment.** Bailey & Iqbal (2008) report pupil-based measurements and transient workload decreases at boundaries between task chunks. **Class:** primary HCI experiment and methodological analogy. **Connection:** R008/R012 could compare opportunistic interruption with fixed/immediate policy; prospective human benefits require a separately authorized study. **Value:** medium-high; **confidence:** high identity / moderate abstract-level claim / low warning-safety transfer. **Action:** review as a methodology comparator; do not equate a physiological workload estimator with an authorized warning policy.

**O40 / S142 — age of information (AoI) is not latency, throughput or action validity.** Kaul, Yates & Gruteser (2012) define a recipient-side *time since the currently held status was generated* metric for queued updates; the IEEE abstract reports a status-update optimum that differs from maximizing throughput or minimizing packet delay in their model. **Class:** primary information/queueing **methodological tool**, not a human-factors observation. **Connection:** R012 may need to distinguish source event time, message generation, delivery and action deadline; #36's persistent-state channel is a candidate application. **Value:** high for formal precision; **confidence:** high for model claim, low for untested acoustic translation. **Action:** consider a synthetic-only freshness/validity comparator, with explicit retained-event obligations; no new schema requirement.

**O41 / S143 — discarding stale *status samples* can reduce queue staleness, but may erase an event that requires action.** Costa, Codreanu & Ephremides (2016; 2015 author preprint) analyse timestamped updates and packet-management policies and introduce a peak-age metric. **Class:** queueing **methodological tool**; the loss-of-action-event example is **our counter-hypothesis**, **not a result tested in their paper**. **Connection:** R012/R014 and #36 distinguish a replaceable state sample from non-replaceable event/action messages, even when the latter has an older timestamp. **Value:** high; **confidence:** high identity/mathematical motivation, low operational transfer. **Action:** build fictional update-versus-event negative controls; do not adopt queue-drop behaviour or assert the oldest item is always most actionable.

### PERPENDICULAR — opportunity prediction may fail, and removing interruptions is not cost-free

**X43 / S144 — context-tailored timing is not guaranteed to increase a meaningful outcome.** Morrison and ten coauthors (2017) randomised **77** app users to intelligent, fixed-daily and occasional push-notification regimes. The open PLOS results report no meaningful intelligent-versus-daily difference in observed intervention use and no meaningful group difference in percentage viewed. **Class:** task-specific **counterevidence** to universal benefit from adaptive timing; **not** an alarm-safety experiment. **Connection:** R008/R014 should include a transparent fixed-policy comparator, distinguish notification count from proportion viewed and from correct action, and resist biometrics-dependent feature creep. **Value:** high scope control; **confidence:** high original-article reading on named abstract/method/result sections; low transfer. **Action:** use as a null counterexample; source underwent a **2018 affiliation-only correction** (see provenance), not an outcome correction.

**X44 / S145 — batching may improve reported well-being, whereas complete suppression may raise anxiety.** Fitz and colleagues (2019) describe a **237-person randomised field experiment** across usual, batched and absent notifications. Publisher abstract attributes improved stress/attentiveness indicators to some predictable batching and increased anxiety/FoMO to full shutoff. **Class:** source-reported within-domain result and **counterposition** to either "always interrupt immediately" or "suppress all notifications". Consumer-message schedules are not hazard deadlines; the abstract's productivity wording is internally inconsistent, so **no productivity-direction claim is extracted**. **Connection:** R008/R013 alternatives to continuous machine speech; possible *noncritical* communication contrast only. **Value:** medium; **confidence:** high identity / moderate abstract outcome / very low emergency transfer. **Action:** use as a boundary case, not a default batching recommendation.

### Cross-axis test — age, timeliness and action obligation do not have one order

**Hypothesis, not established evidence.** A message can be *fresh but nonactionable* (latest normal state), *old but still action-relevant* (unresolved transient fault requiring inspection), *accurate but too late* (past its action deadline), or *early but addressed to the wrong actor*. A policy that minimizes mean AoI, reduces interruption count or improves median first-response latency can still lose a critical obligation or misstate a now-cleared condition. AoI is a status-freshness **measure**, not a semantic priority or human comprehension score.

### PARALLEL — noticing an omission is not a delivered status message
