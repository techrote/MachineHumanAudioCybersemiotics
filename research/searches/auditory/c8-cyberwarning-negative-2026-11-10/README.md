## A consequential contrary result: detection alone is misleading

Zhang et al. (2024), SAGE HTML **Method > Subjects / Apparatus and Stimuli; Results; Discussion**, report a three-arm, between-subject online experiment. Of 142 initially recruited participants, 113 remained after attention exclusions. Each viewed ten phishing and ten legitimate email images. The 1-second spoken alert said `phishing alert`; the earcon was an approximately 557 Hz tone. Both types of alert were intentionally fallible: alerts sounded for nine of ten phishing and four of ten legitimate images.

In **Results**, the authors report a higher phishing hit rate for the earcon than for speech (means **0.52 vs 0.41**, contrast `p=.034`). However, both alert arms also had higher false-alarm rates than no-audio control (earcon **0.51**, speech **0.50**, control **0.36**; combined alert-versus-control comparison `p=.001`). Crucially, the study reports **no statistically significant between-condition difference in response sensitivity d-prime** (overall `p=.069`), although classification times were shorter for the alert conditions (Welch ANOVA `p=.004`). These values are transcribed from HTML paragraphs, not recalculated, independently checked against figures or endorsed as an effect estimate.

The high-value negative case is a **signal that raises response propensity without clearly establishing improved discrimination**. A high hit rate is not synonymous with reliable comprehension or good decisions when false positives are costly. Conversely, the study cannot establish that earcons are inherently superior: the voice carried a generic two-word message, while the tone differed in pitch and potential urgency. Its authors explicitly contrast their study with a more content-rich voiced warning rated by experts in Cooper et al. (2020), **Discussion, paragraphs 1–3**.

## Different measurements cannot be treated as replications

Datta et al. (2021), Springer HTML **§3.1, §3.2, §5.2 and §5.3**, describe a formative study with **26 sighted college students**, using naturalistic auditory icons to represent phishing, suspicious downloads and form filling. The abstract reports average immediate meaning recall of **58%** and about **73%** correct threat identification in browsing contexts; §5.2 lists in-context identification rates spanning **73–100%** across scenarios. Those are different measurement contexts. The article also explicitly states that the sighted group received **no distractors**, unlike an earlier five-person visually impaired study. Neither observation validates cross-population equivalence.

In **§5.3.1**, reported intentions to close a browser or cancel a download followed *simulated safe scenarios*, not encounters with live malicious websites. A stated protective action must not become a measured prevented attack. The authors' §4.2 heuristics also require separate validation of the *warning trigger*; knowing what a sound signifies is not the same as ground-truth threat accuracy.

Cooper et al. (2020), publisher **Abstract**, describe prototype judgements by 32 cybersecurity/IT specialists. A richer spoken explanation of phishing signs may be preferred over a brief cue, but this is not the same speech stimulus, audience or outcome as Zhang et al.'s student task. The comparison challenges blanket modal rankings without deciding which modality is optimal.
